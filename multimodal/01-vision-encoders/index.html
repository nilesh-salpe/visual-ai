Vision encoders: ViT and CLIP

How images become sequences a transformer can read (patches), and how contrastive training on image-caption pairs gives encoders that line up with language: ViT (Vision Transformer), CLIP and SigLIP.

What is the problem?A language model reads tokens. An image is a grid of millions of pixel values with no tokens, and classic vision models were trained to predict a fixed list of labels, so their features do not connect to the open vocabulary of language.
Core ideaCut the image into patches (16 × 16 pixels in ViT) and treat each as a token: a 224 × 224 image becomes 196 tokens. Train the encoder contrastively against text: CLIP learns to tell which caption goes with which image across a batch, from 400 million internet pairs, so image and text embeddings share one space and classes can be named in words (zero-shot).
1 · Problem

Pixels are not tokens

A 1,000 × 1,000 photo is three million numbers. A language model expects a sequence of a few thousand token embeddings at most.

        ::: cards grid3

[d] No tokens

Images have no natural vocabulary or word boundaries.

[b] Fixed labels

Classic classifiers predict a predetermined set of categories; naming a new concept needs new labelled data.

[c] Separate spaces

Image features and word embeddings were trained apart, so they do not line up. :::

2 · Limitation

What earlier vision models lacked

CNN inductive biases

Convolutions build in locality, but tie vision to an architecture separate from language models.

Label bottleneck

ImageNet's 1.28 million labelled images cover 1,000 classes.

Poor transfer by name

You cannot ask a label classifier about "a corgi wearing sunglasses".

3 · Your turn

How many tokens?

Pick an answer before moving on. Committing to a guess, even a wrong one, makes the real answer stick.

A ViT reads 224 × 224 RGB (red, green, blue) images with 16 × 16 patches. How many patch tokens does one image produce?

B. (224 / 16)² = 14² = 196 patches; each patch flattens to 16 × 16 × 3 = 768 values before the linear projection.
4 · Core idea

Patches, then contrastive alignment

ViT

Split into 16 × 16 patches, linearly embed, add positions and a [class] token, run a standard transformer. Pretrained on ImageNet-21k or JFT-300M, it reached 88.55% on ImageNet with less training compute than comparable CNNs (convolutional neural networks).

CLIP

Train image and text encoders to predict which caption goes with which image in a batch; 400 million internet pairs; evaluated on over 30 datasets.

Zero-shot transfer

Classify by comparing an image with text prompts for each class: CLIP matched the original ResNet-50 on ImageNet without its 1.28M training labels.

SigLIP

A pairwise sigmoid loss needs no batch-wide softmax: 84.5% zero-shot ImageNet with 4 TPUv4 chips in 2 days; batches beyond 32k gave little extra benefit.

5 · How it works

Worked example: zero-shot classification with CLIP

Classify a photo as dog, cat or car without training a classifier.

Worked example

  1. Write one prompt per class: "a photo of a dog", "a photo of a cat", "a photo of a car", and embed each with the text encoder.
  2. Embed the photo with the image encoder; normalise all vectors.
  3. Compute the cosine similarity between the photo and each prompt; the highest wins.
  4. This is how CLIP matched the original ResNet-50 on ImageNet zero-shot, without using any of its 1.28 million training examples.
With a shared embedding space, a class is just a sentence.
6 · Why it works

Why it works

Language as supervision

Captions name millions of concepts for free; the web supplies the labels.

Shared space

Matching pairs pulled together, mismatches pushed apart, so text can query images and vice versa.

Scale over bias

With enough data, a plain transformer learns the spatial structure CNNs had built in.

Reusable encoders

CLIP-style encoders became the eyes of many vision-language models (deck 02).

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"Vision transformers need convolutions."

ViT is a pure transformer over patches; with large-scale pretraining it matches or beats CNNs.

"CLIP was trained on ImageNet labels."

It was trained on 400M image-text pairs from the internet; its ImageNet result is zero-shot.

"Contrastive learning needs huge batches forever."

SigLIP found benefits diminish quickly; 32k was sufficient.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • Tokens = (H/p) × (W/p). 224 px with 16 px patches → 14 × 14 = 196 patch tokens (+1 class token in ViT).
  • Contrastive = batch classification. Each image must pick its own caption out of the batch, and each caption its image.
  • Sigmoid loss scales easily. SigLIP scores each pair independently, so no batch-wide normalisation is needed.

⚠ Traps

  • Forgetting resolution cost. Halving the patch size quadruples the token count.
  • ViT on small data. ViT shines when pretrained at scale (ImageNet-21k, JFT-300M); from scratch on small data CNNs win.
  • Assuming CLIP reads text in images well. Its strengths and weaknesses vary widely across the 30+ datasets evaluated.
8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Answer from memory. If you can't, revisit the slide named in the answer.

What is CLIP's training objective?

B. A symmetric contrastive loss over the batch.

How does zero-shot classification with CLIP work?

Embed a text prompt per class and the image; predict the class with the highest cosine similarity.

What does SigLIP change?

It replaces the softmax over the batch with an independent sigmoid on each image-text pair, which decouples the loss from batch size.
9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

A vision encoder turns an image into patch tokens; contrastive training puts those tokens in the same space as language.
Say it in 30 seconds

A Vision Transformer splits an image into fixed-size patches (16 × 16 pixels), embeds each with a linear projection, adds positions and runs a standard transformer; a 224 × 224 image is 196 patch tokens. Pretrained at scale, ViT reached 88.55% on ImageNet. CLIP trains an image encoder and a text encoder together to pick the matching caption among a batch, on 400 million image-text pairs; the shared space allows zero-shot classification, matching ResNet-50 on ImageNet without its training labels. SigLIP replaces the softmax with a pairwise sigmoid loss and reached 84.5% zero-shot ImageNet with four TPUv4 chips in two days.

Further reading

Key papers

The primary sources behind this deck, each with what it contributed. Links go to the paper (arXiv or the conference page).

An Image is Worth 16x16 Words: Transformers for Image Recognition at Scale
Dosovitskiy et al. · ICLR 2021

A pure transformer over sequences of image patches (16 × 16 pixels, linearly embedded, with a [class] token); pretrained on ImageNet-21k or JFT-300M it matches or beats CNNs with substantially less training compute.

Remember: 88.55% on ImageNet (best model)
Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision (CLIP)
Radford et al. · ICML 2021

Image and text encoders trained contrastively to predict which caption goes with which image, on 400 million internet pairs; text prompts enable zero-shot transfer to over 30 datasets.

Remember: matches the original ResNet-50 on ImageNet zero-shot, without its 1.28M training examples
Sigmoid Loss for Language Image Pre-Training (SigLIP)
Zhai et al. · ICCV 2023

A pairwise sigmoid loss that needs no batch-wide normalisation; works at small batch sizes and scales up; benefits of batch size diminish, 32k being sufficient.

Remember: 84.5% zero-shot ImageNet with 4 TPUv4 chips in two days (SigLiT)
