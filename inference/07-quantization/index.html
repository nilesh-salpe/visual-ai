Quantization for inference

Fewer bits per weight, activation and KV (key-value) entry: INT8 (8-bit integer) and INT4 (4-bit integer) scales, the activation-outlier problem and SmoothQuant's fix, weight-only methods (GPTQ, AWQ (Activation-aware Weight Quantization)), and FP8 (8-bit floating point).

What is the problem?Decode speed and capacity are limited by bytes: weights and KV cache read every step. 16-bit numbers are expensive to move.
Core ideaStore numbers in 8, 4 or fewer bits with scales, chosen per tensor, per channel or per group, and handle outliers so accuracy survives.
1 · Problem

Bytes, not FLOPs, set decode speed

At small batch, every decode step streams the weights (and the KV cache) from HBM (high-bandwidth memory). Halve the bytes and you roughly halve that time, and twice as much fits in memory.

Weight bytes for a 70B-parameter model at different precisions.

2 · Limitation

Naive rounding fails on LLM activations

One scale for everything

A per-tensor scale must cover the largest value. Everything else gets only a few effective levels.

Activation outliers

A few activation channels are far larger than the rest, so per-tensor INT8 on activations loses the small values (the problem SmoothQuant targets).

Very low bits

Naive 3–4-bit rounding of weights loses accuracy without smarter error handling.

4 · Core idea

Scales, granularity, and where the bits go

q = clip(round(x / s), −qmax, qmax),   x̂ = q · s,   s = max|x| / qmax

Granularity

Per tensor → per channel → per group: smaller groups mean tighter ranges and less error, at the cost of storing more scales.

W8A8 (SmoothQuant)

INT8 weights and activations via smoothing: up to 1.56× speed and 2× less memory with negligible accuracy loss.

Weight-only, 3–4 bits

GPTQ: second-order one-shot quantization; a 175B model in ~4 GPU-hours (GPU = graphics processing unit); 3.25× (A100) and 4.5× (A6000) over FP16 (16-bit floating point). AWQ: protect the ~1% salient weights found from activations; >3× over HF (Hugging Face) FP16.

FP8

E4M3 and E5M2 floating point, with per-tensor or per-block scales. It matched 16-bit training quality in Micikevicius et al. (2022).

At a glance

Quantization trades a little accuracy for fewer bytes to store and to read.

5 · How it works

Worked example: memory and decode speed for a 70B model

70B parameters; group-wise INT4 uses one FP16 scale per 128 weights. Decode at batch 1 reads every weight once per token (H100: 3.35 TB/s).

Worked example

  1. BF16 (bfloat16, a 16-bit floating-point format with the exponent range of 32-bit floats): 70B × 2 bytes = 140 GB; INT8: 70 GB; INT4: 35 GB.
  2. INT4 scale overhead: 16 bits / 128 weights = 0.125 bits per weight → +1.1 GB, so 4.125 effective bits.
  3. Time to read the weights once: BF16 42 ms, INT8 21 ms, INT4 11 ms (bandwidth only, as if on one GPU).
  4. Prefill is compute-bound, so weight-only INT4 helps it far less unless the kernel computes in low precision.
Weight-only INT4 cuts the per-token weight read about 3.9× vs BF16. That speeds memory-bound decode and frees memory for KV cache.
6 · Why it works

Why it works

Fewer bytes, faster decode

Decode is memory-bound, so moving fewer bytes per step speeds it up directly and frees memory for more KV.

Finer scales, smaller ranges

Error is about half a step, and the step is range / levels, so finer granularity means smaller error.

Outliers moved, not removed

SmoothQuant's transform is exactly equivalent and balances activation and weight ranges.

Error-aware rounding

GPTQ compensates rounding errors using second-order information, and AWQ protects activation-salient weights.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"Quantization always costs a lot of accuracy."

With good scales and outlier handling, INT8 (SmoothQuant) and 3–4-bit weight-only (GPTQ, AWQ) report small or negligible losses on large models.

"Weight-only INT4 speeds up everything equally."

It reduces weight bytes, which helps memory-bound decode the most. Compute-bound prefill gains less.

"Per-tensor scales are fine for activations."

LLM activations have outlier channels, so per-tensor activation INT8 needs SmoothQuant-style smoothing or finer scales.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • Effective bits = bits + scale_bits / group_size. INT4 with FP16 scales per 128 → 4.125 bits.
  • Weight-only quantization speeds decode. Decode is bandwidth-bound; fewer bytes per weight = faster steps.
  • Handle outliers. Per-channel scales, SmoothQuant's migration (α = 0.5 default), or keep outlier dimensions in 16-bit (LLM.int8()).

⚠ Traps

  • Per-tensor activation scales. A few outlier channels blow up the scale and crush everything else to zero.
  • Forgetting the scales. Group scales add real memory; group size trades accuracy for overhead.
  • Expecting INT4 compute speedups everywhere. Without low-bit kernels, weights are dequantized and the matmul runs in 16-bit.
  • Treating KV-cache and weight quantization as the same. KV is quantized at runtime per token; errors accumulate differently.

Why is SmoothQuant's transform "free" at inference?

X·W = (X·diag(s)⁻¹)(diag(s)·W). The weight scaling is folded in offline and the activation division can be fused into the previous layer, so the result is unchanged and nothing is added at runtime.

9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

Quantization cuts the bytes that bound decode. Scales at the right granularity, outlier handling (SmoothQuant), error-aware weight methods (GPTQ, AWQ) and FP8 keep accuracy while moving 2–4× fewer bytes.
Say it in 30 seconds

Decode is memory-bound, so storing weights, activations and KV in fewer bits speeds it up and frees memory. Quantization maps values to integers with a scale. Finer scales (per channel, per group) cut error, but LLM activations have outlier channels that break per-tensor INT8. SmoothQuant moves that difficulty into the weights with an exact rescaling, reporting up to 1.56× speed and 2× less memory. GPTQ and AWQ push weights to 3–4 bits. FP8 (E4M3, max 448; E5M2, max 57,344) uses floating point with scales.

Further reading

Key papers

The primary sources behind this deck, each with what it contributed. Links go to the paper (arXiv or the conference page).

LLM.int8(): 8-bit Matrix Multiplication for Transformers at Scale
Dettmers et al. · NeurIPS 2022

Finds systematic outlier features in large transformers and handles them with a mixed-precision decomposition: outlier dimensions in 16-bit, everything else in 8-bit.

Remember: more than 99.9% of values multiplied in 8-bit, no degradation up to 175B
SmoothQuant: Accurate and Efficient Post-Training Quantization for Large Language Models
Xiao et al. · ICML 2023

Migrates quantization difficulty from activation outliers to weights with a mathematically equivalent per-channel scaling, enabling W8A8 (8-bit weights and activations).

Remember: up to 1.56× speedup and 2× memory reduction
GPTQ: Accurate Post-Training Quantization for Generative Pre-trained Transformers
Frantar et al. · ICLR 2023

One-shot weight quantization using approximate second-order information, quantizing one column at a time and compensating the error in the rest.

Remember: 175B model to 3–4 bits in about four GPU hours
AWQ: Activation-aware Weight Quantization for LLM Compression and Acceleration
Lin et al. · MLSys 2024

Weight-only low-bit quantization that protects the most important weight channels, chosen by activation magnitude, by scaling them up before quantizing.

Remember: protecting 1% of salient weights greatly reduces error; >3× over Hugging Face FP16 with its TinyChat kernels
FP8 Formats for Deep Learning
Micikevicius et al. · arXiv 2022

Defines the two FP8 encodings, E4M3 (more precision) and E5M2 (more range), and shows FP8 training and post-training quantization matching 16-bit results.

Remember: E4M3 max 448, E5M2 max 57,344
