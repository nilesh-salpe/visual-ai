GPU failures:
at scale they are routine

With 16,000 GPUs, something breaks every few hours. The question is how quickly you notice, how much work you lose and how fast you resume. This deck covers failure rates, failure types, why a single fault stops the whole job, checkpointing maths, and silent data corruption.

02 · The data

Cluster MTBF = per-GPU MTBF ÷ number of GPUs

Each H100 is quite reliable: the Llama 3 data implies about 50,000 hours (roughly 6 years) between interruptions per GPU. But the job is only as reliable as the product of all its components, so the failure rate grows with the size of the cluster.

03 · Failure types

Reading the signals: Xid errors and other failure modes

The NVIDIA driver logs Xid events to the kernel log (dmesg). Some point to your code, some to the hardware. Click one.

Fail-stop

GPU off the bus, uncorrectable ECC, node crash. Loud and easy to detect.

Network

Link flaps, bad optics or cables, switch reboots. They show up as NCCL timeouts or hangs.

Fail-slow

Thermal throttling, degraded links, a slow host. No error at all, just a slower job.

Silent (SDC)

The arithmetic is wrong and nothing reports an error. The hardest to detect (slide 08).

04 · Blast radius

Why one dead GPU stops 16,383 healthy ones

Synchronous training is a chain of collectives. If one rank stops contributing, every other rank waits inside the all-reduce until a watchdog times out. The job then has to tear down, swap the bad node and restart from the last checkpoint.

The cost of a failure = time to detect + time to restart + work lost since the last checkpoint, multiplied by every GPU in the job. A 10-minute NCCL timeout on 16K GPUs wastes about 2,700 GPU-hours before anything has even been diagnosed.
05 · Blast radius

Stragglers: a slow GPU is worse than a dead one

A dead GPU triggers a restart. A GPU that is 20% slow (thermal throttling, a degraded NVLink, a noisy neighbour on the host) quietly slows every step of the job, and nothing raises an alarm.

Detect stragglers by timing each rank's compute and communication, for example with the NCCL flight recorder, per-rank step-time histograms, or comparing a rank's GEMM TFLOPS with its peers. Then evict the slow node like a dead one.
06 · Recovery

Checkpoint interval: frequent saves vs. lost work

Checkpointing often means more time spent saving. Checkpointing rarely means more work lost per failure. The optimum is about τ* ≈ √(2 · δ · MTBF) (Young/Daly), where δ is the time the training loop is blocked while saving.

Lowering δ is what moves the curve, and async checkpointing does that: snapshot the state to CPU memory in seconds, then write it to storage in the background. Then you can checkpoint every few minutes.

08 · Silent errors

Silent data corruption: the GPU gives a wrong answer and reports nothing

A defective ALU, a marginal voltage or an aging part can flip bits in a computation without triggering ECC. Training continues on bad numbers. Sometimes the loss spikes; sometimes the model just gets subtly worse.

Loss / grad-norm monitors

Flag spikes against a rolling z-score, then roll back and skip the batch.

Cross-replica checks

DP replicas should hold identical weights. Compare checksums periodically.

Deterministic replay

Re-run the suspicious step on other hardware and diff the results.

Known-answer tests

Periodic GEMM tests with known answers, run during the job or in preflight.

Meta, "Detecting silent errors in the wild" (2022); Google, "Cores that don't count" (HotOS 2021). Llama 3 attributed 6 of 419 interruptions to SDC.
09 · Operations

The node lifecycle in a well-run cluster

1 · Burn-in

New nodes run multi-day stress tests (GEMM, HBM, NVLink, NCCL) to catch early failures before they join production.

2 · Preflight

Before each job: dcgmi diag, an NCCL all-reduce check against expected busbw, a GEMM TFLOPS check, and ECC / row-remap counters.

3 · In-job monitoring

Xid watchers, per-rank step-time telemetry, NIC counters, temperature and clocks. Automatic cordon on anomalies.

4 · Quarantine / RMA

Failing nodes are drained, re-tested, repaired or sent back to the vendor. A node returns only after it passes burn-in again.

"How would you design fault tolerance for a 10K-GPU training job?" Cover five layers: prevent (burn-in, preflight), detect (fast, per-rank), contain (cordon, spares), recover (async and in-memory checkpoints, warm restart), verify (SDC checks).
10 · Test yourself

Questions to test your understanding

Your 4K-GPU job hangs at step 30,000 with no error. How do you debug it?
A hang means some rank isn't entering, or isn't finishing, a collective. Steps: (1) Use the NCCL flight recorder or dumps to see which collective each rank is stuck in and which rank is missing. (2) Use py-spy dump on the stuck ranks for the Python stack. (3) Check that node's dmesg for Xid errors, NIC and link counters, and thermal throttling. (4) Look for a code-level cause, such as data-dependent control flow making ranks issue collectives in different orders. Then cordon the node and restart from checkpoint, with a shorter watchdog timeout in future.
How often should you checkpoint a 16K-GPU run?
Use Young/Daly: τ* ≈ √(2δM). With an MTBF of about 3 h and a 5-minute blocking checkpoint, τ* ≈ √(2 × 5 × 180) ≈ 42 minutes. With async checkpointing the stall drops to about 10–30 s, which gives τ* ≈ 10 minutes and much less lost work. Also check storage bandwidth: a 405B model with optimizer state is about 6 TB, so how fast you can write it (and spreading the writes across ranks) matters.
What's the difference between fail-stop, fail-slow and silent failures?
Fail-stop: the component dies and reports an error (Xid 79, uncorrectable ECC), so it's easy to detect and the response is restart. Fail-slow: it works, but degraded (throttling, link down-training), so the whole synchronous job slows and you need per-rank telemetry to find it. Silent (SDC): wrong results with no error. You need numerical monitors, cross-replica checksums and replay. Many teams consider SDC the most dangerous because it can damage the model without anyone noticing.
Why not just make training asynchronous so failures don't block everyone?
Asynchronous or stale-gradient SGD hurts convergence and reproducibility at LLM scale. Synchronous data parallelism with large batches is the proven approach. The industry made synchronous training resilient instead: fast detection, hot spares and cheap checkpoints. There is research on elastic and partial-failure-tolerant training (for example dropping a DP replica for a few steps), but it isn't yet the default.
11 · Take it with you

Four things to remember

Failures scale linearly with GPU count. At 16K H100s, expect one about every 3 hours, mostly from the GPU and HBM.
One fault stops the whole synchronous job, and stragglers slow every step without raising an error.
Optimal checkpoint interval ≈ √(2δ·MTBF). Async and in-memory checkpoints reduce δ, and the lost work along with it.
Prevent, detect, contain, recover, verify, with SDC as the silent risk that needs active checks.
