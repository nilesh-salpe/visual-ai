Heterogeneous GPUs:
getting value from a mixed fleet

Real fleets mix A100s, H100s, H200s, B200s and sometimes other vendors. This deck covers why mixing hurts synchronous training, how to rebalance the work, where mixed hardware actually helps (disaggregated inference), and what breaks along the way.

01 · The fleet

A new GPU generation every ~2 years, and old ones stay in service for 5+

02 · Training

Synchronous training runs at the speed of the slowest GPU

With equal per-GPU batches, every step waits for the slowest rank. Mix A100s into an H100 job and the H100s spend most of each step idle at the all-reduce.

Model: 8 ranks; an A100 is about 3× slower than an H100 at BF16 (bfloat16, a 16-bit floating-point format with the exponent range of 32-bit floats) GEMMs (312 vs 989 TFLOPS (trillion floating-point operations per second) peak; the measured gap is often 2–3×). With proportional batching, gradients must be averaged weighted by sample count.

With equal batches, adding slow GPUs can make the job slower than leaving them out. Uneven batches fix the compute imbalance, but memory limits and the communication pattern still differ between the two types.
03 · Training

Pipeline parallel on mixed GPUs: balance the layers by speed

Put 80 layers on 4 pipeline stages: 2 on H100s and 2 on A100s. With an equal split, the A100 stages become the bottleneck and the pipeline stalls. Rebalance by giving faster stages more layers, as long as each stage's memory still fits.

Heterogeneous pipelines are an optimization problem with two constraints: equalize stage time and stay within each stage's memory. Research systems solve it automatically (Metis, HexiScale, Cephalo), but production labs usually avoid mixing GPU types inside one training job.

Worked example

  1. Each step waits for the A100: the H100 finishes in 32% of the step and idles 68% of the time.
  2. Balance by speed: give each H100 3.2× the work of an A100.
  3. In a 1 A100 : 1 H100 pair, the H100 should get 76% of the batch.
  4. Pipeline parallelism can instead give faster GPUs more layers.
Heterogeneity is fine when the work split follows each GPU's speed (and memory); equal splits waste the faster GPUs.
04 · Inference

Where mixing helps: prefill and decode on different hardware

Prefill is compute-bound and decode is bandwidth-bound (see deck 02). Disaggregated serving runs them on separate GPU pools, each matched to its phase, and sends the KV (key-value) cache between them.

Separating the pools also removes interference: a long prefill no longer pauses everyone's decode. Splitwise, DistServe, Mooncake and NVIDIA Dynamo all use this design. The cost is moving the KV cache between pools, which needs fast links or pipelined transfer.

05 · Gotchas

What breaks when you mix GPUs

Numerics

V100 has no BF16, and A100 has no FP8. A recipe that depends on FP8 Tensor Cores can't run on the older parts, and changing precision changes training dynamics.

Kernels

FlashAttention-3, wgmma and TMA (Tensor Memory Accelerator) are Hopper-only, and Blackwell adds new instructions. You end up maintaining several code paths and checking that they agree numerically.

Communication

NCCL (NVIDIA) and RCCL (AMD) don't interoperate in one collective. Mixed-vendor jobs need separate groups joined by a gateway, or they don't work at all.

Memory shape

40 vs 80 vs 141 vs 192 GB means different shard sizes, batch sizes and KV capacity. One parallelism config no longer fits every GPU.

Reproducibility

Different kernels and reduction orders give results that differ in the last bits. Bitwise reproducibility across generations is effectively impossible.

Operations

Different drivers, firmware, failure modes and topologies (NVLink-8 vs NVL72 vs PCIe (PCI (Peripheral Component Interconnect) Express, the standard expansion bus)). Scheduling has to understand GPU types.

The common pattern in industry: keep each training job on one GPU type, and use the mix at the fleet level, putting each workload on the generation that suits it.
06 · Placement

Matching workloads to GPU generations

Fit scores are judgement calls meant for discussion, not benchmarks. They weigh compute, bandwidth, memory, interconnect and opportunity cost.

Old GPUs aren't useless: they handle evals, data processing, embedding and small-model work well. That frees the newest parts for the jobs that need the largest NVLink domain and FP8 or FP4 (4-bit floating point).
Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • Split work ∝ throughput. Batch share or layer count per GPU type.
  • Prefill on new, decode on old. Decode is memory-bound and runs well on older GPUs (Splitwise).
  • Keep TP (tensor parallelism) within one GPU type. Mixed types inside a TP group run at the slowest.

⚠ Traps

  • Equal batches on unequal GPUs. The fast GPUs idle at every all-reduce.
  • Numerics differences. Different kernels and formats (FP8 support) change results slightly.
  • Interconnect mismatch. Different NVLink generations can't share a domain.
07 · Test yourself

Questions to test your understanding

You have 2K H100s and 2K A100s. How do you use them for one model?
Don't run one synchronous job across both. Equal-batch DP would run at A100 speed, and proportional batching makes memory and communication harder. Better options: (1) run pretraining on the H100s and put evals, data processing, RL (reinforcement learning) rollouts or inference on the A100s; (2) if it must be one job, use separate pipeline stages per type with layers balanced by speed, or weighted DP; (3) for serving, use disaggregation: prefill on the H100s, decode on whichever has the best bandwidth per dollar.
Why does disaggregated prefill/decode make sense?
The two phases have opposite bottlenecks: prefill is compute-bound (needs TFLOPS) and decode is bandwidth- and capacity-bound (needs HBM bandwidth and KV space). Running them together means long prefills stall decode (bad TPOT (time per output token) tail latency) and each batch is a compromise. Splitting them lets you size and choose hardware per phase and tune TTFT (time to first token) and TPOT SLOs (service-level objectives) independently. The cost is moving the KV cache, which has to go over RDMA (remote direct memory access) or NVLink and be pipelined layer by layer.
What changes when you port a training stack from H100 to MI300X?
The software stack changes from CUDA (Compute Unified Device Architecture, NVIDIA's programming platform for its graphics processors) to ROCm (AMD's Radeon Open Compute software stack for its graphics processors)/HIP (AMD's Heterogeneous-computing Interface for Portability) and NCCL to RCCL. Kernels must be ported or come from Triton (FlashAttention has ROCm versions). The parallelism plan changes too: 192 GB of HBM allows larger shards and less TP or PP (pipeline parallelism), and the GPU-to-GPU fabric differs (Infinity Fabric, with a different topology). Budget for numerical validation: loss curves must match the reference within tolerance before you scale up.
08 · Take it with you

Four things to remember

A synchronous job runs at the speed of its slowest GPU. Mixing generations in one job wastes the fast ones unless you rebalance.
Rebalance by speed: batch per GPU in DP, layers per stage in PP, both subject to memory limits.
Disaggregated serving is where mixing pays off: prefill on compute-heavy GPUs, decode on bandwidth-heavy ones.
Use one GPU type per job and mix at the fleet level, putting each workload on the generation that suits it.

Further reading

Key papers

The primary sources behind this deck, each with what it contributed. Links go to the paper (arXiv or the conference page).

Splitwise: Efficient Generative LLM (large language model) Inference Using Phase Splitting
Patel et al. · ISCA 2024

Characterizes prompt computation as compute-intensive and token generation as memory-intensive, and splits them onto separate machines, possibly of different GPU types.

Remember: 1.4× throughput at 20% lower cost, or 2.35× throughput at the same cost and power
DistServe: Disaggregating Prefill and Decoding for Goodput-optimized Large Language Model Serving
Zhong et al. · OSDI 2024

Defines serving quality as goodput, the request rate served within both TTFT and TPOT targets, and runs prefill and decode on separate GPUs so they stop interfering.

Remember: 7.4× more requests or 12.6× tighter SLO than colocated systems
Mooncake: A KVCache-centric Disaggregated Architecture for LLM Serving
Qin et al. · arXiv 2024

Kimi's serving platform: separate prefill and decode clusters with a disaggregated KV cache pool built from CPU (central processing unit), DRAM (dynamic random-access memory) and SSD (solid-state drive) resources.

Remember: 75% more requests on real workloads
