Tokenization for LLMs · 02

Byte-pair encoding, from scratch

The algorithm behind most LLM tokenizers: start from characters and repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair (Sennrich, Haddow and Birch, 2016).

What is the problem?Translation and language modelling are open-vocabulary problems, and a fixed word list can't cover rare words, names and compounds.
Core ideaLearn a vocabulary of frequent character sequences by repeatedly merging the most frequent pair of symbols. Rare words are then spelled with those pieces.
1 · Problem

Rare words are everywhere

Names

New people, places and products appear constantly. Many can just be copied or transliterated piece by piece.

Compounds

German builds words like Abwasserbehandlungsanlage ("sewage water treatment plant"), the paper's own example. It's unlikely to be in any word list, but its parts are common.

Cognates and loanwords

Words shared across languages often translate through regular character-level patterns.

The paper's observation: many rare words can be translated through units smaller than words.
2 · Limitation

Before BPE: shortlists, unknown tokens and back-off dictionaries

Fixed word vocabulary

Models used a shortlist of frequent words; everything else became an unknown token.

Back-off dictionary

Unknown words were looked up in a separate dictionary after translation, outside the neural model. This was the paper's baseline.

Characters only

Fully open, but sequences become much longer, which hurts efficiency and makes information travel further.

4 · Core idea

Merge the most frequent pair, repeat

1. Start from characters

Each word is a sequence of characters plus an end-of-word symbol ·, so the original spacing can be restored.

2. Count adjacent pairs

Count every pair of neighbouring symbols, weighted by word frequency. Pairs never cross word boundaries.

3. Merge the top pair

Replace every occurrence of the most frequent pair (A, B) with a new symbol AB and record the merge.

4. Repeat

Stop after a chosen number of merges. Final vocabulary = initial characters + merges, and the number of merges is "the only hyperparameter".

6 · Why it works

Why BPE works

Frequency decides granularity

Frequent strings are merged early, so common words become single symbols. Rare words remain split into smaller units.

No shortlist, no back-off

Any word made of known characters can be encoded, so the model handles rare and unseen words itself.

Compression

It comes from a data-compression algorithm (Gage, 1994): fewer symbols are needed to encode the text.

Measured gains

On WMT 15, subword models improved over the back-off dictionary baseline by 1.1 BLEU (EN→DE) and 1.3 BLEU (EN→RU) (abstract).

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"BPE finds the shortest or most likely segmentation of a word."

It replays merges greedily, in learned order. That is fast and deterministic, but not an optimization over all segmentations (compare the unigram LM in deck 04).

"You choose the vocabulary size directly."

In the original algorithm you choose the number of merges; vocabulary = characters + merges. Toolkits such as SentencePiece let you specify the final vocabulary size instead (deck 05).

"BPE merges can join two words."

Not in the paper: "we do not consider pairs that cross word boundaries". LLM tokenizers enforce this with pre-tokenization (deck 03).

9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

BPE starts from characters and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair. Replaying those merges in order turns any word into known subword units.
Say it in 30 seconds

Byte-pair encoding, adapted to NLP by Sennrich et al. (2016), learns a subword vocabulary from data. Every word starts as characters plus an end-of-word marker; you repeatedly count adjacent symbol pairs and merge the most frequent one, and the number of merges is the only hyperparameter. Frequent words end up as single symbols and rare words split into pieces, so there are no unknown words. To encode new text you replay the merges in learned order.
