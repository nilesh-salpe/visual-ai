Autograd in PyTorch

How PyTorch records the graph during the forward pass, what .backward() and .grad do, why gradients accumulate, and how no_grad, detach, frozen parameters and custom Functions fit in.

What is the problem?You need gradients for millions of parameters from arbitrary Python code, sometimes across micro-batches, sometimes with parts frozen, sometimes with custom operations.
Core ideaEvery operation on a tensor with requires_grad=True records a grad_fn node. loss.backward() walks the graph in reverse and adds gradients into each leaf's .grad. torch.no_grad() stops recording, detach() cuts a tensor out of the graph, and torch.autograd.Function lets you define your own backward.

4 · Core idea

The pieces of autograd

Graph recording

Ops on tensors with requires_grad=True attach a grad_fn (e.g. MulBackward0) linked to their inputs.

backward()

Starts from a scalar, walks grad_fn nodes in reverse and accumulates into leaf .grad fields.

Stopping gradients

torch.no_grad() / inference_mode() for evaluation; detach() for a graph-free view; requires_grad_(False) to freeze.

Custom Functions

Subclass torch.autograd.Function with forward/backward and ctx.save_for_backward; check with gradcheck.

5 · How it works

Reading the recorded graph

For y = (torch.tanh(x) * 2).sum(), following y.grad_fn.next_functions prints (notebook 02):

SumBackward0
  MulBackward0
    TanhBackward0
      AccumulateGrad      ← writes into x.grad

Memory: what is saved

TanhBackward saves tanh(x) (its output) because d tanh = 1 − tanh²; MulBackward saves the other operand. These saved activations are what makes training memory-hungry and what activation checkpointing recomputes (Distributed deck 08).

5 · How it works

Worked example: accumulating over micro-batches

A batch of 32 examples that does not fit in memory at once; split into k = 4 micro-batches of 8 (notebook 02).

Worked example

  1. For each micro-batch: loss = mse(model(xᵢ), yᵢ) / 4, then loss.backward(). Do not zero gradients in between.
  2. Because .backward() adds into .grad, after 4 calls each .grad holds the sum of the four scaled gradients.
  3. That equals the gradient of the mean loss over all 32 examples: the notebook checks every parameter matches to 10⁻⁶.
  4. Forgetting the ÷ 4 gives a gradient 4× too large; forgetting zero_grad() before the next step adds the previous batch's gradient as well.
Accumulation is how large effective batch sizes fit in limited memory, and it relies on .grad adding up.
6 · Why it works

Why define-by-run works

Graphs follow the code

The graph is rebuilt every forward pass, so Python control flow just works.

Accumulation is a feature

Adding into .grad enables micro-batching and shared weights (tied embeddings).

Precise control

Freezing and detaching make LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation), distillation targets and EMA (exponential moving average) models easy.

Compilable

torch.compile captures the same graph ahead of time for speed (deck 07).

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"backward() replaces the gradient."

It adds to .grad; you must zero it between optimizer steps.

"no_grad() freezes parameters."

It only stops recording inside the block; freezing is requires_grad_(False) on the parameters.

"detach() copies the tensor."

It returns a view sharing the same storage, without graph history.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • zero_grad(set_to_none=True). Default in recent PyTorch; saves memory.
  • Divide by k when accumulating. Keeps the gradient equal to the mean.
  • gradcheck in float64. Validates custom backward functions.

⚠ Traps

  • In-place ops on saved tensors. Raise "modified by an inplace operation" at backward.
  • Calling backward twice. The graph is freed after the first call unless retain_graph=True.
  • Using .data. Bypasses autograd silently; use detach() or no_grad().
8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Answer from memory. If you can't, revisit the slide named in the answer.

Which call frees the graph after use by default?

loss.backward() (unless retain_graph=True).

How do you emulate a batch of 64 with micro-batches of 16?

Call backward on each of 4 micro-batch losses divided by 4, without zeroing between them, then step once.

What must a custom autograd Function provide?

A forward that computes the output (saving tensors with ctx.save_for_backward) and a backward returning the gradient for each input.
9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

Autograd records a grad_fn graph during the forward pass; backward() walks it in reverse and adds into .grad, so zero gradients each step and divide by k when accumulating.
Say it in 30 seconds

When a tensor requires gradients, every operation on it records a backward node, so the forward pass builds a graph that mirrors your Python code. Calling backward on the loss walks that graph in reverse and adds each parameter's gradient into its dot-grad field. Because gradients add, training loops zero them every step, and gradient accumulation divides each micro-batch loss by the number of micro-batches to reproduce a larger batch exactly. No-grad blocks stop recording, detach removes a tensor from the graph, freezing turns off requires-grad, and custom Functions define their own backward.
