Mixed precision in PyTorch

How torch.autocast decides which operations run in BF16 (bfloat16, a 16-bit floating-point format with the exponent range of 32-bit floats) or FP16 (16-bit floating point), why weights and reductions stay in FP32 (32-bit floating point), and how GradScaler protects FP16 training from overflow and underflow.

What is the problem?Low-precision math is much faster on Tensor Cores, but some operations lose too much precision or overflow in 16 bits. Choosing per operation by hand is impractical.
Core ideaWrap the forward pass in torch.autocast(device_type, dtype): matmuls and linear layers run in low precision, while reductions, softmax and losses stay in FP32, and the stored weights stay FP32. For FP16, torch.amp.GradScaler multiplies the loss, skips steps with inf/NaN (not a number) gradients and adapts the scale; BF16 usually needs no scaler.

2 · Limitation

Why not run everything in 16 bits?

Accumulation

Long sums lose small terms (the stall above).

Range (FP16)

Values above 65,504 overflow and tiny gradients underflow to 0.

Weight updates

lr × gradient is often smaller than a BF16 weight's spacing; updates vanish without FP32 masters.

4 · Core idea

Autocast + (for FP16) GradScaler

Low-precision ops

matmul, linear, conv, SDPA (scaled dot-product attention) run in the autocast dtype.

FP32 ops

Reductions (sum), softmax, log, losses and norms stay in FP32 for accuracy.

GradScaler

scale(loss).backward(); step(opt) skips if inf/NaN; update() halves or grows the scale.

BF16 shortcut

BF16 has FP32's exponent range, so most BF16 training uses autocast without a scaler.

5 · How it works

The FP16 recipe in code

scaler = torch.amp.GradScaler("cuda")          # init_scale 65536, growth 2, backoff 0.5, interval 2000
for x, y in loader:
    opt.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
    with torch.autocast("cuda", dtype=torch.float16):
        loss = model(x, y)
    scaler.scale(loss).backward()
    scaler.unscale_(opt)                                 # so clipping sees true gradients
    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
    scaler.step(opt)                                     # skipped if any grad is inf/NaN
    scaler.update()

# BF16: same loop without the scaler lines.
5 · How it works

Worked example: what autocast changes (measured)

Notebook 06 on CPU (central processing unit) with torch.autocast("cpu", dtype=torch.bfloat16); defaults reported by PyTorch 2.14.1.

Worked example

  1. Inside autocast: a @ b and nn.Linear outputs are bfloat16; a.sum() and F.softmax outputs stay float32; the Linear's weight is still float32.
  2. Adding 0.001 ten thousand times: an FP32 total reaches 10.0004; a BF16 total stalls at 0.5, because near 0.5 BF16 values are 2⁻⁸ ≈ 0.004 apart and 0.001 rounds away.
  3. GradScaler defaults: initial scale 65,536, growth ×2 every 2,000 clean steps, backoff ×0.5 on overflow.
  4. In the notebook, an injected inf loss leaves the weights unchanged (step skipped) and halves the scale; two clean steps later it doubles again.
Autocast picks precision per op; FP32 is kept exactly where accumulation and range matter.
6 · Why it works

Why it works

Speed where it counts

Matmuls dominate FLOPs (floating-point operations) and run at Tensor Core rates in BF16/FP16.

Accuracy where it matters

Reductions and master weights stay in FP32.

Automatic

Op lists maintained by PyTorch replace error-prone manual casting.

Self-tuning scale

GradScaler keeps FP16 gradients in range without manual tuning.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"Autocast converts the model to half precision."

Weights stay FP32; ops cast their inputs as they run.

"You always need GradScaler."

It is for FP16; BF16's 8-bit exponent avoids most underflow.

"A skipped step means training is broken."

Occasional skips are how dynamic scaling finds the largest safe scale.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • Autocast only the forward + loss. Backward follows automatically.
  • Default autocast dtypes. CUDA (Compute Unified Device Architecture, NVIDIA's programming platform for its graphics processors) float16, CPU bfloat16; pass dtype=torch.bfloat16 on GPUs (graphics processing units) that support it.
  • scaler.unscale_(opt) before clipping. Otherwise you clip scaled gradients.

⚠ Traps

  • Calling .half() on the model for training. Loses the FP32 master weights.
  • Clipping scaled gradients. Unscale first.
  • Autocast around the optimizer step. Not needed and can change update precision.

Check yourself

GradScaler's default initial scale?

65,536 (2¹⁶).

Why does autocast keep softmax in FP32?

Exponentials and normalising sums are sensitive to precision and range; FP32 avoids overflow and rounding errors.

What happens when GradScaler finds inf in the gradients?

The optimizer step is skipped and the scale is multiplied by the backoff factor (0.5).
9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

torch.autocast runs matmuls in BF16/FP16 but keeps reductions, softmax and the weights in FP32; GradScaler adds loss scaling, step skipping and adaptive scale for FP16.
Say it in 30 seconds

Mixed precision in PyTorch is mostly one context manager. Inside torch autocast, matrix multiplications and linear layers run in bfloat16 or float16, while sums, softmax, losses and normalisations stay in float32, and the stored weights are never changed. The notebook shows why: a total kept in bfloat16 stops growing at one half when you keep adding a thousandth. For float16, a GradScaler multiplies the loss so small gradients survive, skips any step whose gradients overflow, halves its scale when that happens and doubles it after a run of clean steps. Bfloat16 usually needs no scaler.
