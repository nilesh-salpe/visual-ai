Knowledge distillation

Training a small model to imitate a large one: soft targets and temperature, sequence-level distillation from teacher outputs, logit distillation in pretraining (Gemma 2, Llama 3.2), on-policy distillation with reverse KL (Kullback-Leibler divergence), and distilled reasoning models.

What is the problem?Large models are accurate but slow and costly to serve. A small model trained from scratch on the same data is much weaker, partly because a one-hot next-token label says nothing about which other tokens were nearly right.
Core ideaUse the large model as a teacher. The student learns from the teacher's full probability distribution (soft targets, logit distillation) or from its sampled outputs (sequence-level distillation: fine-tune on teacher text). On-policy distillation lets the student generate and has the teacher score every token it wrote (reverse KL), so the student learns on the states it actually reaches.
1 · Problem

Big models are good; small models are what you can afford

Serving cost and latency grow with model size, and many products need a model that runs on one GPU (graphics processing unit) or on a phone. Training a small model from scratch on the same data leaves accuracy on the table.

Labels are thin

A next-token label is one-hot: it says which token came next, not which alternatives were plausible.

Capacity is limited

A small model cannot learn everything in the data, so what it is shown, and how, matters more.

The teacher already knows

A large model's probabilities encode similarity between answers: "Sydney" is a better wrong answer than an unrelated word. Hinton et al. call this the information in the small probabilities.

2 · Limitation

Why simply fine-tuning on teacher text is not enough

One sample per prompt

Sampled teacher text shows one continuation; the teacher's other likely answers are lost.

Train-inference mismatch

A student fine-tuned on teacher text learns on the teacher's states, then generates from its own, where its early mistakes take it off the training distribution (GKD (Generalized Knowledge Distillation)).

Mode covering

Matching the teacher with forward KL pushes a small student to spread probability over everything the teacher allows, including regions it cannot model well (MiniLLM).

3 · Your turn

Distill or run RL?

DeepSeek trained Qwen2.5-32B two ways: large-scale RL (reinforcement learning) directly on the base model, and SFT (supervised fine-tuning) on outputs from DeepSeek-R1. Which scored higher on AIME (American Invitational Mathematics Examination) 2024 (pass@1)?

4 · Core idea

Three ways to learn from a teacher

Soft targets (logit distillation)

Minimise the cross-entropy (equivalently, forward KL) between the teacher's and student's token distributions, both at temperature T, scaled by T², often mixed with the normal label loss. Needs the teacher's logits.

Sequence-level distillation

Kim and Rush: train on the teacher's generated outputs (beam search in translation, samples for LLMs (large language models)). Works with only text access; the best student ran 10× faster than its teacher with little loss in BLEU (Bilingual Evaluation Understudy, an n-gram overlap score for translation).

On-policy distillation

The student samples a response; the teacher scores each of its tokens and the student minimises reverse KL(student ‖ teacher) per token (GKD, MiniLLM). Dense, per-token feedback on the student's own mistakes.

Pruning plus distillation

Cut layers, heads or neurons from a trained model, then recover quality by distilling from the original: Minitron needed up to 40× fewer tokens per model than training from scratch.

5 · How it works

Forward vs reverse KL interactive

A one-dimensional picture of a capacity gap: the student is a single Gaussian, the teacher has two modes. For a language model, "modes" are different plausible continuations.

5 · How it works

Worked example: what a soft target carries

For "The capital of Australia is", a teacher gives Canberra 0.90, Sydney 0.08 and Melbourne 0.02 (illustrative numbers). The one-hot label says only "Canberra".

Worked example

  1. Hard label: the loss is −log q(Canberra). The student learns nothing about Sydney being a closer mistake than an unrelated word.
  2. Soft target at T = 1: the cross-entropy −Σ p log q covers all three tokens, so the student is also pushed to give Sydney about 0.08.
  3. At T = 4 for both models, the teacher's probabilities become proportional to p^(1/4): 0.974, 0.532, 0.376, or 0.52, 0.28, 0.20 after normalising. The small probabilities now carry a visible signal.
  4. Hinton et al. multiply the soft-target loss by T² (16 here), because its gradients scale as 1/T²; this keeps it balanced against the hard-label loss.
A soft target gives the student a full distribution per token instead of one label; temperature makes the small probabilities count.
6 · Why it works

Why it works

More bits per example

A soft target is a whole distribution per token; DistilBERT kept 97% of BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers)'s language understanding with 40% fewer parameters, running 60% faster.

Easier targets

The teacher has already resolved noise and ambiguity in the data, so its outputs are cleaner to learn than raw web text.

Ensembles into one

Hinton et al.: over 80% of the frame-accuracy gain from an ensemble of 10 speech models transferred to a single distilled model.

Learning from your own mistakes

On-policy distillation grades the states the student actually visits, like RL, but with a dense per-token signal instead of one reward per episode.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"Distillation only shrinks a model after training."

Gemma 2 and Llama 3.2 used teacher probabilities during pretraining itself, as the main training signal for the small models.

"A distilled student cannot get anywhere near its teacher."

Gaps remain, but they can be small: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B scored 72.6% on AIME 2024 against 79.8% for R1 itself, and beat OpenAI o1-mini (63.6%).

"You always need the teacher's logits."

Sequence-level distillation needs only generated text (as in the R1 distills); logits allow richer token-level and on-policy distillation.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • Small open models are distilled. Gemma 2 (2B, 9B), Llama 3.2 (1B, 3B) and the small Qwen3 models were all trained against a larger teacher.
  • Reverse KL suits generative students. MiniLLM: forward KL makes the student overestimate the teacher's low-probability regions; reverse KL is mode-seeking.
  • On-policy distillation is cheap. Thinking Machines reached 70% on AIME 2024 with Qwen3-8B-Base at an estimated 9 to 30 times lower cost than extending off-policy SFT.

⚠ Traps

  • Training only on teacher text. The student never sees its own mistakes during training, so errors compound at inference (the mismatch GKD addresses).
  • Copying the teacher's errors. Distillation transfers mistakes and biases too; filter the teacher's outputs (R1 used rejection sampling and checks).
  • Ignoring terms of service. Some providers prohibit using outputs to train competing models (for example Anthropic's commercial terms, section D.4).
8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Answer from memory. If you can't, revisit the slide named in the answer.

Why does Hinton et al.'s soft-target loss get multiplied by T²?

B. Scaling by T² keeps the relative contribution of soft and hard targets roughly unchanged when T changes.

What is the difference between off-policy and on-policy distillation?

Off-policy: the student trains on sequences the teacher (or a dataset) produced. On-policy: the student generates its own sequences and the teacher scores each token, so training covers the states the student reaches at inference.

Why is reverse KL preferred for distilling generative LMs (language models) in MiniLLM?

Forward KL is mode-covering: it penalises the student for missing any teacher mass, so a small student spreads probability into regions the teacher rarely uses. Reverse KL is mode-seeking: the student concentrates on outputs the teacher rates highly.
9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

Distillation trains a small model on what a large model believes, the full distribution or its outputs, so the small model gets far more signal per example than labels alone.
Say it in 30 seconds

Hinton et al. trained a small model on a large model's softened probabilities: temperature T exposes the small probabilities, and the loss is scaled by T². For language models, sequence-level distillation fine-tunes the student on teacher outputs (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B reached 72.6% on AIME 2024, versus 47.0% for RL on the same base), while logit distillation trains on the teacher's token probabilities (Gemma 2, Llama 3.2). On-policy distillation lets the student generate and minimises reverse KL to the teacher on its own tokens, which avoids the train-inference mismatch and matched RL results at a fraction of the cost.

Key papers

The primary sources behind this deck, each with what it contributed. Links go to the paper (arXiv or the conference page).

Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network
Hinton, Vinyals & Dean · NeurIPS workshop 2015

Train a small model on a large model's softened output distribution (softmax with temperature T), minimizing cross-entropy to those soft targets.

DistilBERT, a distilled version of BERT: smaller, faster, cheaper and lighter
Sanh, Debut, Chaumond, Wolf · NeurIPS EMC² workshop 2019

Distillation during pretraining with a triple loss (soft targets, masked language modelling, cosine of hidden states).

Remember: 40% smaller, 60% faster, 97% of BERT's language understanding
Sequence-Level Knowledge Distillation
Kim, Rush · EMNLP 2016

Train the student on the teacher's beam-search outputs instead of matching per-token distributions; removes the need for beam search at test time.

Remember: student 10× faster than its teacher; 13× fewer parameters with pruning, −0.4 BLEU
MiniLLM: On-Policy Distillation of Large Language Models
Gu, Dong, Wei, Huang · ICLR 2024

Replaces forward KL with reverse KL, so the student does not overestimate the teacher's low-probability regions, optimised with an on-policy policy-gradient method.

Remember: students from 120M to 13B parameters
On-Policy Distillation of Language Models: Learning from Self-Generated Mistakes (GKD)
Agarwal et al. · ICLR 2024

Generalized Knowledge Distillation: train the student on its own sampled sequences with teacher feedback, with a choice of divergence; addresses the train-inference distribution mismatch.

Remember: summarisation, translation and GSM8K (Grade School Math 8K) with T5 students
Gemma 2: Improving Open Language Models at a Practical Size
Gemma Team, Google DeepMind · arXiv 2024

The 2B and 9B models are trained with knowledge distillation from a larger teacher instead of next-token prediction.

Remember: 2B on 500B tokens: 67.7 distilled vs 60.3 from scratch (3-benchmark average)
Llama 3.2: Revolutionizing edge AI (artificial intelligence) and vision with open, customizable models
Meta AI · Meta AI blog 2024

The 1B and 3B models were pruned from Llama 3.1 8B and trained with logits from Llama 3.1 8B and 70B as token-level targets during pretraining.

Remember: teachers: Llama 3.1 8B and 70B
Compact Language Models via Pruning and Knowledge Distillation (Minitron)
Muralidharan et al. (NVIDIA) · arXiv (NVIDIA) 2024

Prune depth, width, attention and MLP (multilayer perceptron) of Nemotron-4 15B, then retrain with distillation on under 3% of the original data.

Remember: up to 40× fewer training tokens per model; up to 16% better MMLU (Massive Multitask Language Understanding) than from scratch
DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via Reinforcement Learning
DeepSeek-AI · arXiv 2025

R1-Zero: RL with rule-based accuracy and format rewards directly on a base model; R1 adds cold-start data and a multi-stage pipeline.
