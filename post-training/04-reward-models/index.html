Post-training · 04

Reward models

How human comparisons become a scalar score: the Bradley-Terry model, the pairwise loss, rankings to pairs, and the limits of any learned reward.

What is the problem?"Helpful, honest, harmless" can't be written as a formula. RL (reinforcement learning) needs a number for every sampled answer, and people can't label millions of samples during training.
Core ideaCollect comparisons between answers, then train a model r(x, y) → scalar so that σ(r_w − r_l) matches how often people prefer y_w over y_l. That model scores RL samples automatically.
1 · Problem

Comparisons are easier than scores

Ask five people to score an answer from 1 to 10 and you get five different calibrations. Ask which of two answers is better and they mostly agree. The Bradley-Terry model links reward differences to preference probabilities. Drag the gap.

2 · Limitation

Why not use a hand-written reward or direct scores?

No formula for "helpful"

Unlike a game score, quality depends on the request, the audience and subtle correctness. Any hand-written rule is easy to game.

Absolute ratings are noisy

Labelers disagree on what "7/10" means, and drift over time. Relative judgements are more consistent.

Humans can't be in the loop

PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) samples millions of responses. A learned reward model answers instantly; humans label only a fixed dataset per round.

3 · Your turn

Read a reward model

Pick an answer before moving on. Committing to a guess, even a wrong one, makes the real answer stick.

A reward model scores answer A at 2.0 and answer B at 0.5. Under Bradley-Terry, what is the predicted probability that a person prefers A?

C. σ(2.0 − 0.5) = σ(1.5) = 1/(1 + e^−1.5) ≈ 0.82. Only the difference matters: scores of 102.0 and 100.5 predict exactly the same preference.
4 · Core idea

Architecture and loss

Model

InstructGPT: start from the SFT (supervised fine-tuning) model "with the final unembedding layer removed" and output one scalar for a (prompt, response) pair. They used 6B reward models; 175B RM training "could be unstable".

Loss

loss = −E[log σ(r(x, y_w) − r(x, y_l))]: maximize the likelihood of the observed choices under Bradley-Terry. Only differences are learned, so rewards are often normalized before RL.

Data

For each prompt, sample several model outputs and have labelers rank them. InstructGPT: K = 4 to 9 per prompt; Llama 2: binary comparisons with a strength label, over 1M of them.

loss(θ) = −1/C(K,2) · E(x, y_w, y_l) [ log σ( rθ(x, y_w) − rθ(x, y_l) ) ] (InstructGPT eq. 1)

At a glance

Only reward differences matter; the RM then scores millions of RL samples automatically.

5 · How it works

From a ranking to training pairs

A labeler ranks K responses. Every ordered pair becomes a comparison: K·(K−1)/2 of them. InstructGPT puts all pairs from one prompt in the same batch element, because they are strongly correlated and treating them as independent samples made the RM overfit.

5 · How it works

How good can a reward model get? simulation

From notebook 04: simulated labelers choose with Bradley-Terry noise around a hidden true reward. Even the true reward function only predicts 81% of their choices, which is the ceiling. A learned RM approaches it as comparisons grow.

Real reward models

Real preference data is noisy too: labelers disagree with each other, so held-out RM accuracy well below 100% is expected. Llama 2 trained two reward models, one for helpfulness and one for safety, because the two goals can conflict and a single model had trouble serving both.

5 · How it works

Worked example: one comparison through the loss

The RM scores the labeler's chosen answer 2.0 and the rejected one 0.5.

Worked example

  1. P(chosen preferred) = σ(2.0 − 0.5) = σ(1.5) = 0.818.
  2. Loss for this pair = −log 0.818 = 0.201.
  3. If the RM had it backwards (0.5 vs 2.0): loss = −log σ(−1.5) = 1.701, a much larger gradient.
  4. A ranking of K = 9 answers contributes C(9, 2) = 36 such pairs, grouped in one batch element.
Bradley-Terry turns "which is better?" into a smooth loss on reward differences.
6 · Why it works

Why it works, and where it breaks

It generalizes the labels

The RM learns features of good answers from tens of thousands of comparisons, then scores answers no labeler has seen.

Comparisons carry fine signal

Ranking 4–9 samples gives up to 36 comparisons per prompt, focused on the subtle differences between plausible answers.

It is only a proxy

The RM is accurate where its training data was. Push a policy far from that data and the RM's mistakes get exploited (deck 06).

It must keep up

As the policy improves, its outputs leave the RM's training distribution. Llama 2 and 3 collect fresh comparisons on the newest model each round.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"A reward model outputs a probability or a 1–10 score."

It outputs an unbounded scalar, identified only up to a constant: only differences between answers to the same prompt mean anything.

"A good reward model should agree with labelers 100% of the time."

Labelers disagree among themselves, so even the "true" reward can't predict every label. Accuracy has a noise ceiling.

"Once trained, the reward model can be reused for the whole project."

It goes stale as the policy improves. Iterated recipes retrain it on comparisons of the current model's outputs.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • P(A ≻ B) = σ(r_A − r_B). A gap of 1.5 ≈ 82%.
  • Normalize rewards before RL. Only differences are identified, so the offset is arbitrary.
  • Group a ranking's pairs. They share responses and are correlated.

⚠ Traps

  • Reading rewards as probabilities. They are unbounded scalars.
  • Expecting 100% accuracy. Labelers disagree; there is a noise ceiling.
  • Reusing a stale RM. As the policy improves, its outputs leave the RM's training distribution.

How many pairwise comparisons does one ranking of K = 9 responses give?

Write the reward-model loss and explain why only differences are learned.

−log σ(r(x,y_w) − r(x,y_l)). Adding the same constant to every reward leaves every difference, and therefore the loss, unchanged.

Why did InstructGPT put all pairs from a prompt into one batch element?

The C(K,2) pairs share responses, so they are highly correlated. Shuffling them as independent samples caused overfitting; grouping them is also cheaper (one forward pass per response).
9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

A reward model learns a scalar score from human comparisons via the Bradley-Terry loss −log σ(r_w − r_l), giving RL an automatic but imperfect judge.
Say it in 30 seconds

Helpfulness has no formula, and humans can't score the millions of samples RL needs. So labs collect comparisons, which are more consistent than absolute ratings, often as rankings of four to nine answers per prompt, and train a model that outputs one scalar per response. The Bradley-Terry model says the probability that one answer is preferred is the sigmoid of the reward difference, so the loss is minus log sigmoid of the winner's score minus the loser's. Only differences are learned. Accuracy is capped by labeler disagreement, the model is only reliable near its training data, and it needs fresh comparisons as the policy improves.

Problem: no formula for "good answer"; humans can't score every RL sample.
Core idea: train r(x, y) on comparisons: −log σ(r_w − r_l); only differences matter.
Limits: label noise caps accuracy; it is a proxy that goes stale as the policy moves.
