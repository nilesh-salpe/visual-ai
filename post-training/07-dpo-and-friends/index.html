DPO, KTO and rejection sampling

Simpler routes to the RLHF objective: Direct Preference Optimization turns it into a classification loss, KTO needs only good/bad labels, and rejection sampling fine-tunes on the best of many samples.

What is the problem?PPO-based RLHF needs four models, generation inside the training loop and delicate tuning. Many teams want the benefit of preference data without running online RL.
Core ideaDPO: the RLHF optimum π* ∝ π_ref·er/β lets you write the reward as β·log(π/π_ref), so the Bradley-Terry loss can be applied directly to the policy, with no reward model and no sampling.
1 · Problem

RLHF vs DPO, side by side

Both start from an SFT model and preference pairs and target the same objective. Compare what each pipeline has to run.

2 · Limitation

What makes PPO-based RLHF heavy

Generation in the loop

Every step samples full answers from the current policy: slow, and awkward to run efficiently next to training.

Four models

Policy, reference, reward model and value model must all fit, two of them with optimizer state.

Tuning

β, clipping ε, learning rates for two networks, advantage estimation settings. Instabilities are common.

3 · Your turn

The implicit reward

Pick an answer before moving on. Committing to a guess, even a wrong one, makes the real answer stick.

After DPO training, the policy gives answer y twice the probability the reference gives it (β = 0.1). What implicit reward does DPO assign to y, up to a per-prompt constant?

B. DPO's implicit reward is r̂(x,y) = β·log(π_θ(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)) = 0.1·ln 2 ≈ 0.069. "Your language model is secretly a reward model."
4 · Core idea

The derivation in three lines

1 · Known optimum

The KL-regularized RLHF objective has the closed-form solution π*(y|x) = π_ref(y|x)·exp(r(x,y)/β) / Z(x).

2 · Invert it

Solve for the reward: r(x,y) = β·log(π*(y|x)/π_ref(y|x)) + β·log Z(x). Any policy defines a reward this way.

3 · Plug into Bradley-Terry

In r(y_w) − r(y_l) the β·log Z(x) terms cancel, leaving a loss on the policy alone.

LDPO = −E[ log σ( β·log(πθ(yw)/πref(yw)) − β·log(πθ(yl)/πref(yl)) ) ]
5 · How it works

What the gradient does

The DPO gradient for one pair is (1 − σ(z))·β·(∇log π(y_w) − ∇log π(y_l)), where z is the current implicit-reward margin. Pairs the model already ranks correctly with a large margin get almost no weight; pairs it gets wrong get the most. Drag the margin.

Notebook 07 checks the theory

On a toy 6-response problem with 20,000 simulated preference pairs, DPO converges to π* ∝ π_ref·er/β (KL(π*‖π) ≈ 0.0008), and its implicit rewards match the hidden true rewards up to a constant: [−0.73, 0.20, −1.24, 1.23, −0.24, 0.78] vs [−0.75, 0.25, −1.25, 1.25, −0.25, 0.75]. With only 200 pairs the KL is 0.19: offline data limits it.

6 · Why it works

Why DPO caught on, and its limits

Simple and stable

A supervised loss on fixed data: two models (policy and frozen reference), no generation, few hyperparameters (mainly β).

Same target as RLHF

In the ideal case it optimizes "the same objective as existing RLHF algorithms" (the paper), so it is not a weaker objective, just a different optimizer.

Offline data limits it

It learns only from the pairs it is given. If they don't cover what the current policy does, it can't correct those behaviours, which is why labs iterate with fresh on-policy pairs.

Still a proxy

The implicit reward is fit to finite, noisy labels. Pushing β small or training long can overfit the pairs, the DPO version of reward hacking.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"DPO doesn't need a reference model."

It does: both log-ratios are taken against a frozen π_ref (usually the SFT model). The reference defines the implicit reward and the KL anchor.

"DPO has no reward model, so it can't be reward-hacked."

The policy is its own reward model. It can still overfit noisy or narrow preference data and drift in odd directions.

"β in DPO is a learning rate."

β is the KL strength from the RLHF objective: a small β allows larger departures from the reference.
8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Answer from memory. If you can't, revisit the slide named in the answer.

Why does the partition function Z(x) disappear from the DPO loss?

B. Both responses share the same prompt x, so β·log Z(x) cancels in r(y_w) − r(y_l).

What does KTO need instead of preference pairs?

A binary label per output: desirable or undesirable. It optimizes a prospect-theory-inspired utility, so data without paired comparisons can be used.

How did Llama 2 use rejection sampling?

Sample K answers per prompt, take the best by the reward model, fine-tune on those. Only rejection sampling up to RLHF-V4, then PPO applied on top of the rejection-sampling checkpoint.
9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

DPO rewrites the RLHF objective as a classification loss on β·log(π/π_ref), so preference data trains the policy directly, with no reward model or RL loop.
Say it in 30 seconds

PPO-based RLHF needs four models and generation inside training. DPO starts from the known optimum of the KL-regularized objective, the reference policy times exp of reward over beta, and inverts it: any policy implies a reward equal to beta times the log ratio to the reference, plus a per-prompt constant. Plugging that into the Bradley-Terry loss cancels the constant, giving a simple loss on preference pairs that needs only the policy and a frozen reference. Its gradient focuses on pairs the model gets wrong. KTO needs only good-or-bad labels, rejection sampling fine-tunes on the best of several samples, and labs iterate with fresh pairs because DPO only learns from the data it is given.
