Reward hacking and overoptimization

Why optimizing a learned reward model too hard makes the model worse, how to measure "too hard" with KL (Kullback-Leibler divergence), and the mitigations labs use.

What is the problem?A reward model is a proxy trained on limited data. The harder RL (reinforcement learning) or best-of-n pushes on it, the more it finds the RM's blind spots: the proxy score climbs while the real quality falls.
Core ideaTreat optimization as a budget measured in KL from the starting policy. True ("gold") reward rises then falls as KL grows (Gao et al. 2022), so limit KL, refresh the reward model, and prefer rewards that can't be gamed.

2 · Limitation

Why a reward model can be gamed

Trained on a narrow distribution

The RM saw outputs from earlier models. As the policy improves, it produces text the RM has never been checked on.

Spurious features

If longer or more confident answers tended to win in the data, the RM may reward length or confidence itself, even when they don't help.

An optimizer finds every flaw

RL is a search for high-scoring outputs. Any systematic error in the RM is a direction the search will follow. Goodhart's law: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."

Pick an answer before moving on. Committing to a guess, even a wrong one, makes the real answer stick.

You increase n in best-of-n (or train RL longer) against a fixed, imperfect reward model. What does the true quality typically do?

B. Gao et al. find gold reward follows d(α − βd) for best-of-n and d(α − β·log d) for RL, with d = √KL: an initial gain, a peak, then decline, while the proxy score keeps increasing.
4 · Core idea

Measure optimization in KL

KL as the distance travelled

How far the optimized policy is from where it started. Best-of-n has an exact formula: KLbon = log n − (n−1)/n, which grows roughly like log n.

Predictable curves

Gold reward R(d), with d = √KL:
best-of-n: d(α − βd)
RL: d(α − β log d)
The coefficients scale smoothly with the reward model's size.

The KL penalty is a brake

RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback)'s −β·KL term (deck 05) caps how far the policy goes, holding it near the region where the RM is trustworthy.

How Gao et al. measured this

Real human labels are too expensive to sweep, so they used a large "gold-standard" reward model as stand-in ground truth, trained smaller proxy RMs on labels from it, optimized policies against the proxies, and scored the results with the gold RM. That synthetic setup made the rise-then-fall curve measurable across many sizes.

5 · How it works

Worked example: the KL cost of best-of-n

KL_bon = log n − (n − 1)/n (nats), from Stiennon et al.

Worked example

  1. n = 2: log 2 − 1/2 = 0.19.
  2. n = 16: log 16 − 15/16 = 1.84.
  3. n = 1,024: 5.93. KL grows like log n: each doubling of n adds ~0.69 nats.
  4. In notebook 06's simulation, gold reward peaks near n = 8 (KL ≈ 1.2) and falls below its starting value by n = 1,024, while the proxy keeps rising.
Pushing harder costs KL; past the peak, extra proxy score is mostly exploitation.
6 · Why it works

Why the curve turns down

Early: real gains

Near the start, the RM's ranking is mostly right, so picking higher-scoring answers picks better answers.

Later: errors dominate

The biggest proxy scores come increasingly from the RM's mistakes. Selection pressure concentrates on exactly those outputs.

The fit is good

In our toy, d(1.20 − 0.52d) predicts every simulated point within ~0.05, with a peak near KL ≈ 1.4 nats (n ≈ 8).

Lesson

A reward model's score is evidence, not a target. More optimization is not automatically better.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"If the reward model score goes up, the model got better."

Only up to a point. Past the peak, proxy score and true quality move in opposite directions.

"Best-of-n is safe because it doesn't change the model."

It still optimizes against the RM. KLbon = log n − (n−1)/n grows with n, and so does exploitation.

"Reward hacking is a bug in the RL algorithm."

It is a property of optimizing any imperfect proxy. A perfect optimizer hacks harder.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • Measure optimization in KL. Gold vs √KL follows d(α − βd) for best-of-n.
  • Bigger, fresher RMs. Overoptimization coefficients scale smoothly with RM size; refresh data each round.
  • Prefer checkable rewards. Where possible, use verifiers (deck 08).

⚠ Traps

  • RM score up ⇒ better model. Only before the peak.
  • Best-of-n is "safe". It optimizes too; KL grows with log n.
  • Ignoring length. A common exploited feature is simply longer answers.

9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

Optimizing a learned reward model is a KL budget: true quality rises, peaks and falls while the proxy keeps climbing, so cap KL and keep the reward honest.
Say it in 30 seconds

A reward model is a proxy trained on a narrow slice of outputs. Best-of-n sampling and RL are searches for high proxy scores, so the harder they push, the more they find the reward model's mistakes. Gao and colleagues measured this: plotted against the square root of KL from the starting policy, the true reward follows d times alpha minus beta d for best-of-n and d times alpha minus beta log d for RL, rising then falling while the proxy keeps increasing. Best-of-n's KL is log n minus n minus one over n. Mitigations are a KL penalty or early stopping, bigger and regularly refreshed reward models, and verifiable rewards where possible.

Problem: the RM is a proxy; optimization finds its errors (Goodhart).
Core idea: measure optimization in KL; gold reward vs √KL rises then falls.
Fixes: KL penalty, early stopping, bigger and fresher RMs, verifiable rewards, monitoring.
