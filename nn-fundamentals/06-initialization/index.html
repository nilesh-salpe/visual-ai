Initialization

Why the random scale of the starting weights decides whether a deep network trains at all, and how Xavier and He initialisation keep signals at a constant size.

What is the problem?Each layer multiplies activations by a random matrix. If that multiplies their size by even 1.1 or 0.9, after 50 layers the signal has exploded or vanished.
Core ideaChoose Var(w) so each layer preserves variance: fan_in × Var(w) × (activation's factor) = 1. Xavier: Var = 2/(fan_in + fan_out), for tanh-like activations. He: Var = 2/fan_in, for ReLU (rectified linear unit), which zeroes half its inputs.

2 · Limitation

Why scale matters so much

Geometric growth

Variance is multiplied by the same factor every layer: 1.1⁵⁰ ≈ 117, 0.9⁵⁰ ≈ 0.005.

Gradients follow

The backward pass multiplies by the transposed weights, so the same factor applies to gradients.

Symmetry

Starting with equal weights makes all units identical forever; randomness is required.

4 · Core idea

A variance budget per layer

The rule

Var(out) ≈ fan_in · Var(w) · Var(in) · c, where c = 1 for linear/tanh near 0 and ½ for ReLU.

Xavier / Glorot

Var(w) = 2/(fan_in + fan_out): balances forward and backward for tanh-like units.

He / Kaiming

Var(w) = 2/fan_in: exactly preserves variance for ReLU, letting very deep ReLU nets train from scratch.

At a glance

Per-layer factor 1 → stable at any depth; 0.9 or 1.1 → vanishes or explodes geometrically.

5 · How it works

Worked example: 50 ReLU layers of width 256

Notebook 05: push standard-normal inputs through 50 layers h ← ReLU(hW), W 256 × 256, and measure the activation std.

Worked example

  1. Weights with std 1: each layer multiplies the variance by 256 × ½ = 128. After 50 layers the std is 6 × 10⁵².
  2. Xavier (Var = 2/512 = 1/256): factor 256 × (1/256) × ½ = ½ per layer. After 50 layers: 3.7 × 10⁻⁸.
  3. He (Var = 2/256): factor 256 × (2/256) × ½ = 1. After 50 layers: 1.24, the same order as at the start.
  4. tanh with std-1 weights keeps std ≈ 1 only because every unit is saturated at ±1, where gradients are ≈ 0.
Initialisation is a variance budget: match fan_in·Var(w) to the activation and depth stops being a problem at the start of training.
6 · Why it works

Why variance preservation works

Signals stay in range

Activations neither saturate nor vanish, so every layer starts in a useful regime.

Gradients stay in range

The same analysis applies backward, so early layers receive meaningful updates.

Cheap

It only sets the scale of the random draw; no extra computation.

Complements normalisation

Norm layers fix scale during training; good init makes the first steps sane.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"Initialisation only matters for the first few steps."

A bad start can leave units dead or saturated permanently, or make training diverge immediately.

"Any small random numbers are fine."

"Small" must be relative to fan-in and activation; too small vanishes just as surely as too large explodes.

"With normalisation layers init is irrelevant."

It matters less, but residual-branch scaling still affects stability of deep transformers.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • He: std = √(2/fan_in). ReLU family.
  • Xavier: std = √(2/(fan_in + fan_out)). tanh / linear.
  • Scale residual branches by ~1/√(2L). GPT-2-style init for deep stacks.

⚠ Traps

  • All-zero (or identical) weights. Every unit gets the same gradient: symmetry is never broken.
  • Ignoring the activation. Xavier is half the right variance for ReLU.
  • Only checking the forward pass. Saturation can keep activations bounded while gradients die.

What happens if all weights start equal?

All hidden units compute the same thing and get the same gradient, so they stay identical: the layer acts like one unit.

Why does ReLU need twice the weight variance of a linear layer?

It zeroes half the inputs, halving the second moment of its output.
9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

Initialise so each layer preserves variance (He 2/fan_in for ReLU, Xavier for tanh); otherwise signals and gradients explode or vanish geometrically with depth.
Say it in 30 seconds

Each layer multiplies activations by a random matrix, so their variance is multiplied by fan-in times the weight variance times a factor from the activation. Repeated fifty times, any factor other than one explodes or vanishes geometrically, and gradients behave the same way. Xavier initialisation sets the variance to two over fan-in plus fan-out for tanh-like units; He initialisation uses two over fan-in for ReLU, which zeroes half its inputs. Weights must also be random to break symmetry. Transformers add tricks such as scaling residual output projections by one over the square root of the depth.

Core idea: fan_in·Var(w)·c = 1: Xavier, He.
Transformers: std ≈ 0.02, residual scaling 1/√N.

Understanding the difficulty of training deep feedforward neural networks
Glorot & Bengio · AISTATS 2010

Studies how activations and gradients shrink or grow across layers and proposes normalized (Xavier) initialization, Var(w) = 2/(fan_in + fan_out).

Delving Deep into Rectifiers: Surpassing Human-Level Performance on ImageNet Classification
He et al. · ICCV 2015

Derives the initialization Var(w) = 2/fan_in for ReLU networks (He/Kaiming init), enabling very deep rectified models to train from scratch; also introduces PReLU (parametric ReLU).
