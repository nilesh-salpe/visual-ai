From MLP to language model

Everything in this topic assembled: embeddings, an MLP (multilayer perceptron) and a softmax over the next character, trained by cross-entropy. Then the step from a fixed context window to the transformer.

What is the problem?We want a model that, given some text, assigns a probability to every possible next token, and that we can sample from to generate more text.
Core ideaFrame language modelling as classification of the next token. A bigram model just counts pairs. A neural LM (Bengio et al., 2003) embeds the last k tokens, passes them through an MLP and outputs a softmax, trained with cross-entropy. Transformers replace the fixed window with attention over the whole prefix.

2 · Limitation

What counting can't do

Tiny context

A bigram sees one previous token; longer n-grams need exponentially many counts.

No sharing

Counts for "cat sat" say nothing about "dog sat", even though they behave alike.

Unseen combinations

Any context never seen in training gets no information beyond smoothing.

4 · Core idea

Language modelling as classification

Inputs

The previous k tokens, each embedded with a shared table and concatenated.

Network

A hidden layer (or many) computes features of the context.

Output

Softmax over the vocabulary; train with cross-entropy on the actual next token, at every position.

At a glance

Transformer: same objective, but attention reads the entire prefix instead of a fixed k.

5 · How it works

Worked example: notebook 07's tiny character model

A repeated toy text of sentences about cats, dogs and frogs: 18 distinct characters; 90% for training, 10% for validation.

Worked example

  1. Uniform guess over 18 characters: ln 18 = 2.89 nats per character.
  2. Counting bigram model (previous character only, smoothed): validation loss 1.00 nats.
  3. Neural model with 3 characters of context (8-dim embeddings, 64 tanh units): 0.33 nats, perplexity ≈ 1.4.
  4. Sampling at temperature 0.8 from "the" produces text like "the cat on the dog sat and the cat still. the log. the frog."
More context and learned features cut the loss threefold; scaling the same recipe (with attention) gives modern LLMs.
6 · Why it works

Why this recipe scales

Self-supervised

Every token of raw text is a training example; no labels needed.

One objective

Next-token cross-entropy forces the model to learn grammar, facts and reasoning patterns that help prediction.

Parallel training

With teacher forcing, all positions of a sequence are trained at once.

Generation for free

Sampling from the predicted distribution and appending turns the predictor into a generator.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"An LLM is fundamentally different from a next-character MLP."

The objective and training loop are the same; architecture and scale differ.

"Language models look up answers in their training data."

They compute a probability distribution from learned parameters; memorisation happens, but generation is computed.

"Lower training loss on a tiny repeated text means a good model."

It mostly means memorisation; validation on held-out text is what counts.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • LM = next-token classifier. Cross-entropy at every position.
  • Teacher forcing. Train on true prefixes; all positions in parallel.
  • Generate autoregressively. Sample, append, repeat.

⚠ Traps

  • Evaluating on training text. Repetitive data makes loss look far better than it is.
  • Fixed context windows. The MLP LM cannot use anything older than k tokens.
  • Greedy sampling. Repetitive loops; use temperature / top-p (Math deck 05).

9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

A language model classifies the next token: embeddings plus a network plus a softmax, trained with cross-entropy; transformers replace the fixed window with attention over the whole prefix.
Say it in 30 seconds

Language modelling is next-token classification. The simplest model counts how often each token follows each other token. Bengio and colleagues' neural model embeds the last few tokens, passes them through a hidden layer and outputs a softmax over the vocabulary, trained with cross-entropy; learned embeddings let it generalise to contexts it never saw. Generation samples a token, appends it and repeats. In notebook 07 this cuts the loss from 1.0 nats for a bigram to 0.33. Transformers keep exactly this objective but use attention to read the whole prefix instead of a fixed window.

Further reading

Key papers

The primary sources behind this deck, each with what it contributed. Links go to the paper (arXiv or the conference page).

A Neural Probabilistic Language Model
Bengio et al. · JMLR 2003

The first neural language model: learned word embeddings plus a network that outputs a softmax over the next word, trained by maximum likelihood.

Attention Is All You Need
Vaswani et al. · NeurIPS 2017

The Transformer: attention-only encoder-decoder with post-norm residual blocks, sinusoidal positions and a ReLU (rectified linear unit) FFN (feed-forward network). The baseline every later change is measured against.

Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners (GPT-2)
Radford et al. · OpenAI 2019

Decoder-only language model that moved layer normalization to the input of each sub-block (pre-norm) and added a final norm.
