Neural Networks · 08

Regularization and generalization

Why a model can fit its training data perfectly and still fail on new data, the classic tools that prevent it (weight decay, dropout, early stopping), and the surprises of modern over-parameterised networks.

What is the problem?Training minimises loss on the data you have, but what matters is loss on data you have not seen. Flexible models can memorise noise instead of learning the pattern.
Core ideaGeneralization gap = test loss − train loss. Weight decay penalises large weights, dropout randomly removes units during training, early stopping stops before memorisation. Large networks can fit even random labels, and test error can fall again past the interpolation point (double descent), so capacity alone does not predict overfitting.

2 · Limitation

Why training loss can mislead

Noise is learnable

A flexible model can fit the noise in each example, which does not repeat in new data.

Finite data

Fine-tuning sets are often thousands of examples for billions of parameters.

Leaky evaluation

Tuning against the test set overfits the benchmark itself (Evals deck 05).

4 · Core idea

Tools that limit memorisation

Weight decay

Shrink weights every step (L2 / AdamW decay): smooth functions are preferred.

Dropout

Zero each unit with probability p in training and scale survivors by 1/(1 − p); averages many thinned networks (Srivastava et al., 2014).

Early stopping

Keep the checkpoint with the best validation loss.

More (and cleaner) data

The most reliable fix; LLM (large language model) pretraining relies on it by rarely repeating tokens.

At a glance

LLMs mostly sidestep overfitting by seeing each token about once; fine-tuning on small data is where it bites.

5 · How it works

The modern picture

Random labels

Zhang et al. (2017): standard networks fit completely random labels, so they have enough capacity to memorise; yet on real labels they generalise.

Double descent

Belkin et al. (2019): test error rises toward the point where the model just interpolates the data, then falls again as it grows larger.

In deep networks

Nakkiran et al. (2020): double descent appears with model size and with training time; near the threshold, more data can even hurt.

Where LLMs sit

Pretraining usually sees each token about once, so train and validation loss track each other closely. Overfitting shows up in fine-tuning on small datasets, in many-epoch training, and in benchmark contamination.

5 · How it works

Worked example: polynomials on 15 noisy points

Notebook 06: y = sin(3x) + noise, 15 training points, 500 test points; least-squares polynomials of growing degree.

Worked example

  1. Degree 3: test MSE (mean squared error) 0.073, close to the noise level.
  2. Degree 14 passes exactly through all 15 points (train error ≈ 0), but its test MSE is 12,756,989: it wiggles wildly between the points.
  3. Adding a tiny ridge penalty λ = 10⁻³ (weight decay for linear models) to degree 14: test MSE 0.102.
  4. Zhang et al. showed standard image networks can fit completely random labels, so for deep nets the story is more subtle: implicit regularisation from SGD (stochastic gradient descent), architecture and data matter.
Capacity without constraints memorises noise; a small penalty on weight size restores generalisation.
6 · Why it works

Why regularisation works

Prefers simple functions

Small weights mean small slopes and gentle curves between data points.

Robust features

Dropout stops units relying on specific partners, so features work in many combinations.

Implicit regularisation

SGD noise and architecture choices bias training toward solutions that generalise.

Data beats tricks

With enough fresh data, memorisation is impossible and the gap closes.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"Overfitting means the model is too big."

Double descent shows bigger can generalise better; it depends on data size, noise and regularisation.

"Dropout is used in all modern LLMs."

Many large pretraining runs use little or no dropout because data is not repeated; it is more common in fine-tuning.

"Zero training loss is the goal."

It is evidence of memorisation unless validation loss is also low.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • Watch the gap. Validation loss rising while training loss falls = overfitting.
  • Dropout scales by 1/(1 − p) in training. Nothing changes at inference.
  • More data is the best regulariser. LLM pretraining rarely repeats data.

⚠ Traps

  • Tuning on the test set. Keep a separate validation split; the test set is for the final number.
  • Dropout at inference. Must be turned off (eval mode).
  • Assuming bigger always overfits more. Double descent: past the interpolation threshold, larger models can generalise better.

9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

Generalisation is about unseen data: penalise weight size, drop units, stop early and above all use more data; over-parameterised nets complicate the classic story with double descent.
Say it in 30 seconds

A model is trained on one dataset but judged on new data, and the difference between the two losses is the generalisation gap. Flexible models can memorise noise: a degree-fourteen polynomial through fifteen noisy points has zero training error and a huge test error. Weight decay penalises large weights, dropout randomly removes units during training and rescales the rest, and early stopping keeps the best validation checkpoint; more data is the strongest fix. Deep networks complicate this: they can fit random labels, and test error can fall again beyond the interpolation threshold, which is called double descent.

Problem: low train loss ≠ low test loss.
Core idea: weight decay, dropout, early stopping, data.
Modern view: random-label fitting; double descent.

Further reading

Key papers

The primary sources behind this deck, each with what it contributed. Links go to the paper (arXiv or the conference page).

Dropout: A Simple Way to Prevent Neural Networks from Overfitting
Srivastava et al. · JMLR 2014

Randomly drops units during training, sampling an exponential number of thinned networks; at test time one unthinned network approximates their average.

Understanding deep learning requires rethinking generalization
Zhang et al. · ICLR 2017

Standard networks easily fit completely random labels, so classical capacity measures cannot explain why they generalize on real data.

Reconciling modern machine learning practice and the bias-variance trade-off
Belkin et al. · PNAS 2019

The "double descent" curve: test error rises as models approach the interpolation threshold, then falls again as they grow past it.

Deep Double Descent: Where Bigger Models and More Data Hurt
Nakkiran et al. · ICLR 2020

Double descent appears in deep networks as a function of model size and of training epochs; near the threshold, more data can even hurt.
