Reasoning and test-time compute · 03

Verifiers and search

Using trained models to score candidate solutions: outcome vs process reward models, best-of-N and step-level search, and allocating test-time compute by problem difficulty.

What is the problem?When answers cannot be checked automatically, choosing among many sampled solutions needs a judge. Scoring only final answers rewards lucky guesses with wrong reasoning, and spending the same compute on every question wastes it on easy ones.
Core ideaTrain a verifier to score solutions. An outcome reward model (ORM) scores the final answer; a process reward model (PRM) scores every step, which supervises reasoning directly and lets you search step by step (beam search, trees). Then spend compute adaptively: more samples or search on hard prompts, less on easy ones.

2 · Limitation

Problems with judging answers only

Sparse signal

An outcome label says the solution failed, not where.

False positives

Correct answers from incorrect reasoning are rewarded and reinforced.

Uniform budgets

Easy prompts are solved on the first try; hard ones need far more compute than an even split gives them.

3 · Your turn

Outcome or process?

Pick an answer before moving on. Committing to a guess, even a wrong one, makes the real answer stick.

Which supervision gives a verifier the most direct signal about where a solution went wrong?

B. Process supervision labels every step, so the reward model learns to spot the first wrong step; Lightman et al. found it significantly outperformed outcome supervision on MATH.
4 · Core idea

Verifiers, step scores and adaptive compute

Outcome verifiers (ORM)

Cobbe et al.: generate many candidates, select the one ranked highest by a trained verifier; verification scaled better with data than finetuning.

Process reward models (PRM)

Score each step. PRM800K: 800,000 human step labels; the PRM solved 78% of a representative MATH subset; active learning improved label efficiency.

Search over steps

Keep the best partial solutions by PRM score and extend them: beam search, or trees of thoughts with look-ahead and backtracking.

Compute-optimal scaling

Choose the method and budget per prompt by difficulty: over 4× more efficient than best-of-N; FLOPs-matched (FLOP = floating-point operation), it beat a 14× larger model where the small model had non-trivial success.

5 · How it works

Worked example: best-of-N with an imperfect verifier

A problem where each sampled solution is right with probability 0.2; N = 16 samples.

Worked example

  1. Coverage: 1 − 0.8¹⁶ ≈ 97% of such problems have at least one right solution.
  2. A verifier with score noise 0.5 (relative to the gap between right and wrong) picks a right one about 84% of the time (notebook simulation).
  3. A noisier verifier (noise 1.5) picks right only about 43% of the time.
  4. Majority voting with errors spread over 10 answers: about 48%.
The verifier's quality, not the number of samples, decides how much of the coverage you keep.
6 · Why it works

Why process supervision and adaptivity help

Dense credit

Step labels say exactly which step failed, a richer training signal than one bit per solution.

Prune early

Scoring partial solutions lets search drop bad branches before spending tokens on them.

Difficulty varies

Easy prompts saturate quickly; hard prompts benefit from search or revisions; very hard ones may need a bigger model.

Alignment benefit

Rewarding correct steps, not just answers, reinforces reasoning a human would endorse.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"Best-of-N with a reward model scales without limit."

Reward models are imperfect: with many samples, the top-scored candidate increasingly exploits the verifier's mistakes.

"A verifier must be as large as the generator."

It can be useful at any size; what matters is how well it separates right from wrong solutions.

"Test-time compute replaces a bigger model."

Only partly: Snell et al. found it beat a 14× larger model on problems the small model could sometimes solve, not on the hardest ones.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • Process beats outcome supervision. Lightman et al.: a PRM solved 78% of a representative MATH subset.
  • Match compute to difficulty. Compute-optimal allocation was over 4× more efficient than best-of-N.
  • Small model + search can beat a big model. On problems with non-trivial base success, FLOPs-matched, it outperformed a 14× larger model.

⚠ Traps

  • Over-optimising the verifier. With many samples, best-of-N finds solutions that fool the reward model.
  • Lucky answers. Outcome-only labels reward correct answers reached by flawed reasoning.
  • Same budget for all prompts. Easy prompts gain nothing from extra search.
8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Answer from memory. If you can't, revisit the slide named in the answer.

What does a process reward model score?

B. It scores steps, which also enables step-level search.

What is compute-optimal test-time scaling?

Allocating test-time compute per prompt (method and amount) according to estimated difficulty, rather than the same best-of-N budget everywhere.

Why can best-of-N get worse with a weak verifier as N grows?

More samples give more chances for a wrong solution to receive a spuriously high score, so the selector picks verifier errors.
9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

A verifier converts samples into accuracy; a process verifier also tells you which step went wrong.
Say it in 30 seconds

Cobbe et al. introduced GSM8K (Grade School Math 8K) and trained verifiers to rank many candidate solutions, which scaled better with data than finetuning alone. Lightman et al. compared outcome supervision with process supervision (labels for each step, 800,000 of them in PRM800K): the process-supervised reward model solved 78% of a representative MATH subset. Snell et al. showed the best use of test-time compute depends on difficulty: allocating it per prompt was over 4× more efficient than best-of-N, and could beat a 14× larger model at equal FLOPs on problems the small model could sometimes solve.

Further reading

Key papers

The primary sources behind this deck, each with what it contributed. Links go to the paper (arXiv or the conference page).

Training Verifiers to Solve Math Word Problems
Cobbe et al. · arXiv 2021

Introduces GSM8K (8.5K grade-school math word problems) and trained verifiers: generate many candidate solutions and pick the one the verifier ranks highest; verification scales better with data than a finetuning baseline.

Let's Verify Step by Step
Lightman et al. · ICLR 2024

Process supervision (feedback on each reasoning step) significantly outperforms outcome supervision for training reward models on MATH; active learning improves it further; releases PRM800K.

Remember: 78% of a representative MATH test subset; 800,000 step-level labels
Scaling LLM (large language model) Test-Time Compute Optimally can be More Effective than Scaling Model Parameters
Snell et al. · ICLR 2025

The best way to spend test-time compute (search against process verifiers, or revising responses) depends on prompt difficulty; allocating it adaptively per prompt is compute-optimal.

Remember: over 4× more efficient than best-of-N; FLOPs-matched, beats a 14× larger model where the small model has non-trivial success
Tree of Thoughts: Deliberate Problem Solving with Large Language Models
Yao et al. · NeurIPS 2023

Search over intermediate "thoughts" with self-evaluation, lookahead and backtracking instead of a single left-to-right chain.

Remember: Game of 24: 74% vs 4% with chain-of-thought (GPT-4)
Large Language Monkeys: Scaling Inference Compute with Repeated Sampling
Brown et al. · arXiv 2024

Coverage (problems solved by any sample) scales with the number of samples over four orders of magnitude, often log-linearly; with automatic verifiers it becomes accuracy, while majority voting and reward models plateau beyond several hundred samples.

Remember: SWE-bench (SWE = software engineering) Lite: 15.9% (1 sample) → 56% (250 samples) vs 43% single-sample state of the art
