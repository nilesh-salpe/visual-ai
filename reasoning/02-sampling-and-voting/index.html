Reasoning and test-time compute · 02

Sampling, voting and coverage

Spending more inference compute by sampling many reasoning paths: self-consistency (majority voting), coverage that keeps rising with samples, and why picking the right sample is the real bottleneck.

What is the problem?Greedy decoding gives one chain of thought, and one wrong step ruins it. Many problems are solved by some sample when you draw enough of them, but you still have to decide which answer to return.
Core ideaSample many chains of thought at temperature above zero. Self-consistency returns the most common final answer (majority vote). Coverage, the share of problems solved by any sample, keeps growing roughly log-linearly with the number of samples; turning coverage into accuracy needs a way to select: votes, a verifier, or an automatic check such as unit tests.
1 · Problem

One chain, one chance

Greedy decoding produces a single reasoning path. If any step goes wrong, the answer is wrong, even though a different path would have worked.

Fragile paths

Long chains have many places to slip.

Many valid routes

Hard problems usually admit several ways of reasoning to the same answer.

Unused compute

Inference typically makes one attempt, even when more attempts would be cheap compared with a bigger model.

2 · Limitation

More samples, but which answer?

No ground truth at inference

For most questions you cannot check an answer automatically.

Correlated mistakes

If most wrong chains share the same mistake, the wrong answer gets the most votes.

Plateaus

Large Language Monkeys found majority voting and reward models plateau beyond several hundred samples, while coverage keeps growing.

3 · Your turn

Coverage or accuracy?

Pick an answer before moving on. Committing to a guess, even a wrong one, makes the real answer stick.

With 250 samples per issue, DeepSeek-Coder-V2 solved 56% of SWE-bench (SWE = software engineering) Lite issues versus 15.9% with one sample. Why could this gain be realised?

B. In code, tests act as an automatic verifier, so any correct sample can be selected: coverage becomes accuracy.
4 · Core idea

Sample, then select

Self-consistency

Sample diverse reasoning paths and return the most consistent final answer: GSM8K (Grade School Math 8K) +17.9, SVAMP +11.0, AQuA +12.2, StrategyQA +6.4, ARC-Challenge (ARC = AI2 Reasoning Challenge) +3.9 points.

Coverage scales

The share of problems solved by any sample grows with the number of samples over four orders of magnitude, often log-linearly.

Automatic verifiers

Unit tests, proof checkers and exact-answer checks select a correct sample when one exists.

Learned verifiers

Without automatic checks, a trained model scores candidates (deck 03).

5 · How it works

Worked example: when does voting help?

Each sampled chain is right with probability 0.3.

Worked example

  1. Wrong chains scatter over 10 different wrong answers: each wrong answer gets about 0.07 of the votes, the right one 0.3, so with 25 samples the majority is right most of the time (the notebook measures above 60%).
  2. Wrong chains all agree on one wrong answer (a common misconception): that answer gets 0.7 of the votes and wins almost every time.
  3. Voting amplifies whatever answer is most likely, right or wrong.
  4. With an automatic checker (run the unit tests), any correct sample can be returned: accuracy approaches coverage, 1 − 0.7²⁵ ≈ 99.98%.
Voting needs errors to disagree with each other; verification needs a reliable checker.
6 · Why it works

Why sampling works

Errors disagree

There are many ways to be wrong and usually one way to be right, so wrong answers split the vote.

Marginalising over paths

Voting approximates the most probable final answer across reasoning paths, not the most probable single path.

Compute as a dial

More samples trade inference compute for accuracy without changing the model.

Parallel

Samples are independent, so they can run in parallel; latency grows less than cost.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"More samples always means a better answer."

Only coverage keeps rising; without a good selector, accuracy plateaus.

"Majority voting fixes systematic errors."

If the model is consistently wrong, voting makes the wrong answer more certain.

"Sampling is free."

N samples cost N times the output tokens; batching helps throughput, not cost.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • Coverage = pass@N. The share of problems where at least one sample is right.
  • Verify when you can. Code and proofs can be checked automatically, so repeated sampling pays off directly.
  • Sample with temperature. Greedy decoding returns the same chain every time.

⚠ Traps

  • Voting on free-form answers. Majority voting needs answers that can be compared (numbers, options).
  • Expecting voting to scale forever. Majority voting and reward models plateau beyond several hundred samples.
  • Ignoring cost. N samples cost about N times the tokens.
8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Answer from memory. If you can't, revisit the slide named in the answer.

Which selector turns coverage into accuracy most directly?

C. A reliable checker accepts any correct sample, so accuracy approaches coverage.

When does majority voting hurt?

When wrong samples concentrate on the same wrong answer, so it outvotes the correct one.

What is coverage?

The fraction of problems solved by at least one of the generated samples (pass@N).
9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

Sampling more solutions raises the chance one is right; the hard part is knowing which one.
Say it in 30 seconds

Instead of one greedy chain of thought, sample many and aggregate. Self-consistency takes the majority answer and improved GSM8K by 17.9 points. Coverage, the share of problems that any sample solves, grows log-linearly over four orders of magnitude of samples: on SWE-bench Lite, DeepSeek-Coder-V2 went from 15.9% with one sample to 56% with 250 when tests could verify the fixes. Without automatic checks, majority voting and reward models plateau after several hundred samples.

Coverage: any sample right; grows with N
Bottleneck: selecting the right sample

Further reading

Key papers

The primary sources behind this deck, each with what it contributed. Links go to the paper (arXiv or the conference page).

Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models
Wang et al. · ICLR 2023

Sample diverse reasoning paths and return the most consistent final answer (marginalising over paths) instead of greedy decoding.

Remember: GSM8K +17.9, SVAMP +11.0, AQuA +12.2, StrategyQA +6.4, ARC-Challenge +3.9 points
Large Language Monkeys: Scaling Inference Compute with Repeated Sampling
Brown et al. · arXiv 2024

Coverage (problems solved by any sample) scales with the number of samples over four orders of magnitude, often log-linearly; with automatic verifiers it becomes accuracy, while majority voting and reward models plateau beyond several hundred samples.

Remember: SWE-bench Lite: 15.9% (1 sample) → 56% (250 samples) vs 43% single-sample state of the art
Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models
Wei et al. · NeurIPS 2022

A few exemplars with intermediate reasoning steps make large models much better at arithmetic, commonsense and symbolic reasoning; the ability emerges around 100B parameters and helps most on multi-step tasks with flat scaling curves.

Remember: GSM8K: PaLM 540B 18% → 57% with 8 exemplars (prior best 55%)
Evaluating Large Language Models Trained on Code (Codex, HumanEval)
Chen et al. · arXiv 2021

HumanEval's 164 hand-written problems scored by unit tests, and the unbiased pass@k estimator.

Remember: Codex-12B: 28.8% pass@1, 70.2% with 100 samples
