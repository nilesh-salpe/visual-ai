Hallucination and factuality

Why language models state plausible falsehoods, how factuality is measured (TruthfulQA, FActScore, SimpleQA), how to detect invented facts from the model's own samples, and what actually reduces them.

What is the problem?Models produce fluent, specific statements that are false, or that the prompt and supplied documents do not support. Readers cannot tell them from correct statements, and most benchmarks score a confident guess higher than "I don't know".
Core ideaSeparate two problems: factuality (false about the world) and faithfulness (contradicts the instruction or the supplied context). Measure correct, incorrect and abstained answers separately; detect invented facts by disagreement between samples; reduce them by grounding (retrieval, quotes, citations), verification steps, and grading that rewards abstention instead of guessing.
1 · Problem

Fluent, specific and wrong

Kalai et al. asked a state-of-the-art open model for one author's birthday, adding "if you know, just respond with DD-MM". Three attempts gave three different wrong dates: 03-07, 15-06 and 01-01.

Looks like a fact

Hallucinations are fluent and specific, so a reader cannot tell them from correct statements.

Two kinds

Factuality: false about the world (a wrong date, an invented citation). Faithfulness: contradicts the instruction, the supplied context or itself (a summary that adds a claim).

Worse on rare facts

FActScore for ChatGPT biographies fell from 80% to 16% as the people written about became rarer.

2 · Limitation

Why it does not go away on its own

Rare facts cannot be learned

A fact seen once is weak evidence. Kalai et al.: after pretraining, the hallucination rate is at least the share of facts that appear exactly once (the singleton rate); if 20% of birthdays appear once, expect at least 20% errors on birthdays.

Grading rewards guessing

Most benchmarks give 1 for correct and 0 for both wrong and "I don't know", so a guess always scores at least as well as abstaining, and models are tuned to these scores.

Imitating human errors

TruthfulQA (817 questions, 38 categories): the best 2021 model was truthful on 58% vs 94% for people, and the largest models were generally the least truthful, because they reproduce popular misconceptions.

3 · Your turn

Which model hallucinates less?

Pick an answer before moving on. Committing to a guess, even a wrong one, makes the real answer stick.

On SimpleQA, GPT-4o was correct on 38.2% of questions, incorrect on 60.8% and declined 1.0%. Claude 3.5 Sonnet was correct on 28.9%, incorrect on 36.1% and declined 35.0%. Which statement is right?

B. Correct-given-attempted was 38.0% for GPT-4o and 44.5% for Claude 3.5 Sonnet. The paper's F-score (harmonic mean of correct and correct-given-attempted) was 38.4 vs 35.0. Which model you want depends on what a wrong answer costs you.
4 · Core idea

Measure, detect, reduce

Measure

Short answers: grade each as correct, incorrect or not attempted (SimpleQA: 4,326 questions, each with one indisputable answer). Long answers: split into atomic facts and report the share a knowledge source supports (FActScore).

Detect without labels

Sample the answer several times. SelfCheckGPT checks whether the other samples support each sentence; semantic entropy groups samples by meaning (bidirectional entailment) and measures uncertainty over meanings rather than wordings.

Ground

Put the source in the context (retrieval), ask for word-for-word quotes before the answer on long documents, and require a citation for every claim, retracting claims without one (Claude documentation).

Verify and allow abstention

Chain-of-Verification drafts an answer, plans verification questions, answers them independently and revises: Wikidata list precision 0.17 → 0.36 for Llama 65B. Give explicit permission to say "I don't know".

5 · How it works

Guess or abstain? calculator

Kalai et al. propose adding the target to the instructions of existing benchmarks ("answer only if you are more than t confident, since mistakes are penalized t/(1 − t) points"), with t = 0.5, 0.75 or 0.9 as natural choices. t = 0 is ordinary binary grading.

5 · How it works

Accuracy hides confident errors paper table

SimpleQA (Wei et al. 2024, Table 3): share of questions answered incorrectly, and share not attempted. Correct: GPT-4o 38.2%, o1-preview 42.7%, Claude 3.5 Sonnet 28.9%, Claude 3 Haiku 5.1%. The questions were collected adversarially against GPT-4o.

Calibration: does the model know what it knows?

Large pretrained models are well calibrated on multiple-choice and true/false questions in the right format, and can estimate P(True) for their own sampled answers (Kadavath et al. 2022). Calibration is what makes abstention work: a model can only "answer if p > t" if its p is honest. Kalai et al. show GPT-4's calibration histograms before and after reinforcement learning: the pretrained model is well calibrated, and post-training can move it away from calibration.

5 · How it works

Worked example: grading with a confidence target

A model is 40% sure of an answer. The benchmark either gives 1 point for a correct answer and 0 otherwise, or states a confidence target t = 0.75 as Kalai et al. propose: "answer only if you are more than 75% confident; mistakes cost t/(1 − t) = 3 points".

Worked example

  1. Binary grading: guessing earns 0.4 × 1 = 0.4 expected points; "I don't know" earns 0. Guessing wins at any confidence above zero.
  2. With the stated penalty: guessing earns 0.4 × 1 − 0.6 × 3 = −1.4; abstaining earns 0. Abstaining wins.
  3. Break-even: p − (1 − p) · t/(1 − t) = 0 gives p = t, so the model should answer exactly when its confidence is above 0.75.
  4. The same rule (answer if p > t) is optimal for every t, so one well-calibrated model can follow whatever target the prompt states.
Binary scoring makes guessing optimal; a stated penalty makes abstaining optimal below the confidence target.
6 · Why it works

Why these fixes work

Errors get a cost

Counting abstentions separately, or penalising errors, turns a confident wrong answer from a free guess into a loss.

Consistency tracks knowledge

A fact stored in the weights comes out the same across samples; an invented detail changes from sample to sample.

The source does the remembering

With retrieval and quotes, the model copies and cites instead of recalling a rare fact. It can still misread the source, a faithfulness error, so check citations.

Independent checks

CoVe answers each verification question without seeing the draft, so it does not simply repeat the draft's mistakes.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"Retrieval-augmented generation eliminates hallucination."

It reduces it. In FActScore, search-augmented PerplexityAI scored 71.5% vs 58.3% for ChatGPT, still leaving 28.5% of atomic facts unsupported; the model can also ignore or misread what it retrieved.

"Hallucination is a mysterious flaw that larger models will outgrow."

Kalai et al. derive it from ordinary statistics: facts seen once cannot be learned reliably, and grading that rewards guessing keeps it after post-training. On TruthfulQA the largest models were generally the least truthful.

"Detecting hallucinations needs access to the model's internals."

SelfCheckGPT works from sampled text alone: if other samples do not support a sentence, it is probably invented.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • Report three numbers. Correct, incorrect and not attempted (SimpleQA); accuracy alone hides confident errors.
  • Score long answers by atomic facts. FActScore: the share of atomic facts in a generation that a knowledge source supports.
  • Sample disagreement is a free detector. Facts the model knows come out the same across samples; invented ones vary (SelfCheckGPT, semantic entropy).

⚠ Traps

  • Treating retrieval as a cure. Search-augmented PerplexityAI scored 71.5% on FActScore: 28.5% of its atomic facts were still unsupported.
  • Penalising "I don't know". Binary-graded leaderboards reward guessing, so optimising for them trains models to bluff.
  • Fine-tuning on facts the model does not know. Gekhman et al.: such examples are learned slowly and, once learned, linearly increase hallucination.
8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Answer from memory. If you can't, revisit the slide named in the answer.

Under 0/1 grading, when does a model maximise its expected score by answering instead of abstaining?

B. A guess earns p ≥ 0 and abstaining earns 0. With a stated penalty of t/(1 − t) per error, answering pays only when p > t.

What is the difference between a factuality and a faithfulness hallucination?

Factuality: the claim is false about the world. Faithfulness: the output contradicts the user's instruction, the provided context, or itself (Huang et al.).

How does SelfCheckGPT detect hallucinations without a knowledge base?

It samples more responses to the same prompt and checks whether each sentence of the main answer is supported by them; sentences the samples do not support are likely hallucinated.
9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

A model hallucinates when guessing scores better than saying "I don't know": fix the incentive, ground the answer, and count errors separately from abstentions.
Say it in 30 seconds

Hallucinations are plausible statements that are false (factuality) or unsupported by the prompt or context (faithfulness). Kalai et al. argue they arise as ordinary prediction errors, at least as often as the share of facts seen only once in training, and persist because binary-graded benchmarks reward guessing. On SimpleQA, GPT-4o attempted almost everything and was wrong on 60.8% of questions, while Claude 3.5 Sonnet declined 35% and was wrong on 36.1%. Measure correct, incorrect and abstained separately, detect invented facts by sample disagreement, and reduce them with retrieval, quotes and citations, verification steps and scoring that rewards abstention.

Two kinds: factuality (the world) vs faithfulness (prompt and context)
Cause: rare facts, plus grading that rewards guessing
Measure: correct · incorrect · abstained; atomic facts
Reduce: ground, verify, reward abstention

Further reading

Key papers

The primary sources behind this deck, each with what it contributed. Links go to the paper (arXiv or the conference page).

Why Language Models Hallucinate
Kalai, Nachum, Vempala, Zhang · arXiv (OpenAI) 2025

Hallucinations arise as ordinary classification errors (at least the singleton rate for arbitrary facts) and persist because binary-graded benchmarks reward guessing; proposes explicit confidence targets with penalty t/(1 − t).

Remember: answer only if confidence > t; errors cost t/(1 − t) points
A Survey on Hallucination in Large Language Models: Principles, Taxonomy, Challenges, and Open Questions
Huang et al. · ACM TOIS 2025

Taxonomy: factuality hallucination (factual inconsistency, fabrication) vs faithfulness hallucination (instruction, context and logical inconsistency); causes, detection and mitigation.

Remember: two primary types: factuality and faithfulness
TruthfulQA: Measuring How Models Mimic Human Falsehoods
Lin, Hilton, Evans · ACL 2022

Questions some people answer falsely because of misconceptions; larger models were generally less truthful.

Remember: 817 questions, 38 categories; best model 58% truthful vs 94% human
FActScore: Fine-grained Atomic Evaluation of Factual Precision in Long Form Text Generation
Min et al. · EMNLP 2023

Splits a generation into atomic facts and reports the share supported by a knowledge source; automated estimator for large-scale evaluation.

Remember: biographies: InstructGPT 42.5%, ChatGPT 58.3%, PerplexityAI 71.5%; ChatGPT 80% → 16% as entities get rarer
Measuring short-form factuality in large language models (SimpleQA)
Wei et al. · arXiv (OpenAI) 2024

Short fact-seeking questions with one indisputable answer, collected adversarially against GPT-4o; graded correct, incorrect or not attempted.

Remember: 4,326 questions; GPT-4o 38.2% correct / 60.8% incorrect; Claude 3.5 Sonnet 28.9% / 36.1% with 35.0% not attempted
SelfCheckGPT: Zero-Resource Black-Box Hallucination Detection for Generative Large Language Models
Manakul, Liusie, Gales · EMNLP 2023

Detects hallucinated sentences by checking whether stochastically sampled responses support them (BERTScore, QA, n-gram, NLI (natural language inference) or LLM-prompt variants); needs no output probabilities or external database.

Remember: black-box: sampled text only
Semantic Uncertainty: Linguistic Invariances for Uncertainty Estimation in Natural Language Generation
Kuhn, Gal, Farquhar · ICLR 2023

Clusters sampled answers by meaning with bidirectional entailment and computes entropy over meanings; more predictive of QA accuracy than token-level baselines.

Remember: unsupervised; one off-the-shelf model
Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know
Kadavath et al. · arXiv (Anthropic) 2022

Larger models are well calibrated on multiple-choice and true/false questions in the right format; models can estimate P(True) for their own answers and be trained to predict P(IK).

Remember: P(True) self-evaluation improves when the model sees several of its own samples
Chain-of-Verification Reduces Hallucination in Large Language Models
Dhuliawala et al. · arXiv (Meta AI (artificial intelligence)) 2023

Draft, plan verification questions, answer them independently, then revise; reduces hallucination on list questions, closed-book QA and long-form generation.

Remember: Wikidata list precision 0.17 → 0.36 (Llama 65B, two-step CoVe)
Does Fine-Tuning LLMs on New Knowledge Encourage Hallucinations?
Gekhman et al. · EMNLP 2024

Fine-tuning examples with knowledge the model lacks are learned much more slowly than known ones and, once learned, linearly increase hallucination; early stopping mitigates it.

Remember: models acquire new facts through pretraining, mostly not fine-tuning
Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP (natural language processing) Tasks
Lewis et al. · NeurIPS 2020

Combines a seq2seq generator with a dense retriever over Wikipedia (non-parametric memory), trained end to end.

Reduce hallucinations (Claude documentation)
Anthropic · Claude platform docs 2026

Allow "I don't know", extract word-for-word quotes first for documents over 20k tokens, require citations and retract unsupported claims, compare several runs.

Remember: quote-first grounding for long documents (>20k tokens)
