Evaluating LLMs · 04

Error bars and comparing models

How much of an eval score is noise: standard errors, clustered questions, paired comparisons between models, and how many questions you need.

What is the problem?Leaderboards rank models by gaps of a point or two, but an eval is a finite sample of questions, and those gaps are often smaller than the sampling error.
Core ideaReport a standard error with every score; use clustered standard errors when questions come in groups; compare models with a paired test on per-question differences; size evals with power analysis.
1 · Problem

How wide is the error bar?

95% confidence interval half-width for an accuracy-style score: 1.96·√(p(1−p)/n). Move the number of questions and the accuracy.

2 · Limitation

Three common statistical mistakes

No error bars

Reporting 71.3 vs 70.1 with no uncertainty invites over-reading a gap that may be noise.

Ignoring clusters

Ten questions about one passage, or one problem in ten languages, are not ten independent samples. Treating them as independent understates the error.

Comparing separate intervals

"The intervals overlap, so no difference" throws away the fact that both models answered the same questions, which a paired test exploits.

3 · Your turn

Pick an answer before moving on. Committing to a guess, even a wrong one, makes the real answer stick.

Model B scores 2 points higher than model A on the same 1,000 questions. Each score alone has a 95% interval of about ±2.8 points. What is the right next step?

C. Both models saw the same questions, so most of the noise (which questions are hard) is shared. The per-question differences have much smaller variance; in notebook 02's simulation, a ~3-point gap is detected 88% of the time with a paired test vs 10% by comparing separate intervals.
4 · Core idea

Four tools

Standard error (CLT)

SE (standard error) = √( (1/n) · (1/(n−1)) Σ(sᵢ − μ̂)² ); 95% CI (confidence interval) = μ̂ ± 1.96·SE. Miller recommends this over bootstrapping as the default.

Clustered SE

Sum residuals within each cluster before squaring: SE = (1/n)·√(Σc(Σi∈c(sᵢ − μ̂))²). Report the cluster count next to the question count.

Paired difference

dᵢ = sAᵢ − sBᵢ; test mean(d) with SE = sd(d)/√n. Uses the shared question difficulty to cancel noise.

Power analysis

Questions needed to detect a gap δ (5% test, 80% power): n = ((1.96 + 0.84)·σd/δ)². Halving δ needs 4× the questions.

At a glance

Treat every eval as a sample of questions.

5 · How it works

Clusters can triple the error

Miller 2024, Table 4 (Anthropic models on real evals): clustered vs naive standard errors on the same data.

Why clusters matter

Questions sharing a passage (DROP, RACE) or a source problem (MGSM's translations) succeed or fail together. The effective number of independent samples is closer to the number of clusters than the number of questions. Miller: "clustered standard errors can be over 3X larger than naive standard errors". Ratios above: DROP 3.05, MGSM 1.88, RACE-H 1.10.

5 · How it works

Worked example: sizing an eval

You want to detect a 2-point improvement. Per-question differences between the two models have standard deviation 0.3.

Worked example

  1. Two-sided 5% test with 80% power: z = 1.96 + 0.84 = 2.80.
  2. n = (2.80 × 0.3 / 0.02)² = 1,766 questions.
  3. For a 1-point gap: 7,064 (≈ 4×).
  4. If questions come in clusters of 10 correlated items, the effective n is closer to the number of clusters: plan for more.
Small gaps need big evals; paired comparisons need far fewer questions than comparing two separate intervals.
6 · Why it works

Why this matters for decisions

Avoid chasing noise

Without error bars, teams "optimize" towards random fluctuations and pick checkpoints by luck.

Get more from the same eval

Paired tests detect real gaps that unpaired comparisons miss, with no extra questions.

Design better evals

Power analysis tells you before building an eval whether it can detect the differences you care about.

Arenas do this too

Chatbot Arena reports confidence intervals on its ratings, so overlapping models can be read as statistically tied (deck 07).

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"Overlapping confidence intervals mean no significant difference."

Not for paired data. Two overlapping intervals can still hide a highly significant paired difference.

"More questions from the same passages give proportionally more precision."

Correlated questions add less information. Use clustered standard errors and count clusters.

"Bootstrapping is always needed for evals."

For mean scores the central-limit-theorem standard error is standard and simpler; Miller recommends it as the default.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • n ∝ 1/δ². Halving the gap needs four times the questions.
  • Pair whenever possible. Same questions for both models → test the differences.
  • Report clusters. Give the cluster count with clustered SEs.

⚠ Traps

  • Overlapping CIs ⇒ no difference. Not for paired data.
  • Treating grouped questions as independent. Clustered SEs can be over 3× naive ones.
  • Chasing noise. Picking checkpoints by sub-SE differences selects luck.

9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

Every eval score needs an error bar: CLT (central limit theorem) standard errors, clustered when questions are grouped, paired tests for model comparisons, and power analysis to size the eval.
Say it in 30 seconds

An eval is a finite sample of questions, so its score has sampling error: at 70 percent on a thousand questions the 95 percent interval is about plus or minus 2.8 points. Report the central-limit-theorem standard error with every score. When questions come in correlated groups, like many questions about one passage, use clustered standard errors, which on real evals can be over three times larger. When comparing two models on the same questions, test the per-question differences: shared difficulty cancels, so a paired test detects gaps that overlapping intervals hide. And use power analysis: detecting half the gap needs four times the questions.

Problem: leaderboard gaps are often smaller than the sampling error.
Tools: CLT SE, clustered SE, paired differences, power analysis.
Numbers: ±2.8 pts at n = 1,000; clustered SE up to 3× naive; n ∝ 1/δ².
