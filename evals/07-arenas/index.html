Evaluating LLMs · 07

Arenas and ratings

Ranking models from crowdsourced head-to-head votes: the Bradley-Terry model behind Chatbot Arena, how it compares with online Elo, and why leaderboards need confidence intervals.

What is the problem?Static benchmarks miss what real users ask and value. Pairwise human votes on live prompts capture it, but they are noisy, unevenly distributed across models, and need a principled way to become a ranking.
Core ideaCollect anonymous pairwise votes on user prompts, fit Bradley-Terry strengths by maximum likelihood (P(i beats j) = σ(ξᵢ − ξⱼ)), and publish ratings with confidence intervals.
1 · Problem

How an arena works

Real prompts

Users bring their own questions, so the distribution reflects actual use, and new prompts keep arriving, so there's no fixed test set to overfit.

Anonymous pairs

Two models answer the same prompt; the user votes for the better one (or a tie) before model names are revealed.

Ratings from votes

Chatbot Arena collected "over 240K votes" in the period covered by its paper and turns them into ratings with a statistical model.

2 · Limitation

Turning votes into a ranking is not trivial

Not everyone plays everyone

Some pairs get thousands of votes, others few. Win rates against different opponents aren't directly comparable.

Votes are noisy

Users disagree and prompts vary in difficulty, so any rating has uncertainty that must be reported.

Online Elo depends on order

Chess-style Elo updates after each game, so the same votes processed in a different order give different ratings, and K controls how much recent votes dominate.

3 · Your turn

Read a Bradley-Terry rating

On the Elo-style scale (400/ln 10 ≈ 174 points per log-odds unit), model A is rated 100 points above model B. Roughly how often should A win a head-to-head vote?

B. P(A wins) = 1/(1 + 10^(−100/400)) ≈ 0.64. On this scale, 100 points is a modest edge, and 400 points means about 10:1 odds.
4 · Core idea

Bradley-Terry vs online Elo

Bradley-Terry (maximum likelihood)

Fit all strengths ξ jointly to all votes: maximize Σ log σ(ξwinner − ξloser). The result doesn't depend on vote order, uses every comparison, and comes with standard errors. Chatbot Arena estimates BT (Bradley-Terry) coefficients and reports intervals via a sandwich estimator and bootstrap.

Online Elo

After each vote, move the winner up and the loser down by K·(1 − expected score). Cheap and incremental, but sensitive to K and to the order of votes, and with no built-in uncertainty.

P(i beats j) = σ(ξᵢ − ξⱼ) = 1 / (1 + 10−(Rᵢ − Rⱼ)/400) with R = 400·ξ / ln 10

At a glance

Order-independent ratings with honest uncertainty.

5 · How it works

Read the intervals, not just the ranks

Overlap means "statistically tied"

If two models' intervals overlap heavily, their order may flip with more votes. Rank bands are more honest than strict ranks.

Intervals shrink with votes

Popular models get many votes and tight intervals; new models start uncertain. Arena's paper also studies sampling pairs to converge faster.

Style and length matter

Human voters, like LLM (large language model) judges, may prefer longer or better-formatted answers, so arenas measure user preference, not correctness alone.

Combine with benchmarks

Arenas capture preference on real prompts; benchmarks test specific skills with known answers. Use both.

5 · How it works

Worked example: what rating gaps mean

Ratings on the Elo-like scale: R = 400 × ξ / ln 10, where ξ is the Bradley-Terry strength.

Worked example

  1. 100-point gap: P(win) = 1/(1 + 10−100/400) = 64%.
  2. 400-point gap: 91% (10:1 odds).
  3. Strength gap of 1.0 log-odds = 400/ln 10 = 174 points = 73% win rate.
  4. Two models 15 points apart with ±20-point intervals are statistically tied.
Read ratings as win probabilities and intervals, not as strict ranks.
6 · Why it works

Why arenas became a standard reference

Hard to overfit

Prompts are fresh and diverse; there's no published test set to train on.

Matches what users feel

Ratings reflect preferences on real tasks, and the Arena paper reports crowdsourced votes agree well with expert raters.

Principled statistics

Bradley-Terry plus confidence intervals turns noisy, unbalanced votes into a ranking with honest uncertainty.

Limits

The user population and its prompts shape the result, preferences include style, and ratings can be gamed by optimizing for what voters like.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"An arena rating is a chess Elo computed game by game."

Chatbot Arena fits Bradley-Terry coefficients by maximum likelihood over all votes, which is order-independent, and reports intervals. It is displayed on an Elo-like scale.

"A higher rank always means a better model."

Neighbouring models often have overlapping intervals. Read rank bands, not strict positions.

"Arena ratings measure correctness."

They measure which answer users prefer, which mixes correctness, helpfulness, style and length.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • P(win) = 1 / (1 + 10^(−Δ/400)). 100 points ≈ 64%.
  • BT = logistic regression on votes. Order-independent; intervals by sandwich estimator or bootstrap.
  • Combine with benchmarks. Arenas measure preference; benchmarks measure specific skills.

⚠ Traps

  • Online Elo. Depends on vote order and K.
  • Strict ranks. Neighbours often overlap.
  • Preference = correctness. Votes include style and length.

9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

Arenas turn anonymous head-to-head votes on real prompts into ratings with Bradley-Terry maximum likelihood, which is order-independent, and should always be read with their confidence intervals.
Say it in 30 seconds

Chatbot Arena shows users two anonymous models answering their own prompt and asks which is better, collecting hundreds of thousands of votes on real, ever-changing prompts that can't be overfit. To rank models from unbalanced, noisy votes it fits the Bradley-Terry model, where the probability that one model beats another is the sigmoid of their strength difference, by maximum likelihood over all votes, and reports confidence intervals. Online Elo, which updates after each vote, depends on vote order and the step size. The ratings are shown on an Elo-like scale where 100 points is about a 64 percent win rate. Arenas measure user preference, including style, so read rank bands and use them alongside benchmarks.
