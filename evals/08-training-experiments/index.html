Evaluating LLMs · 08

Experiments in LLM training

How labs decide between data recipes, architectures and hyperparameters with small, cheap runs: seed noise, low-noise metrics, small-to-large agreement, and efficient hyperparameter search.

What is the problem?A full pretraining run costs too much to try every idea, so decisions are made from small runs. But a small run's benchmark score moves by as much as the effect being tested when only the random seed changes, and the best recipe at 150M parameters is not always the best at 1B.
Core ideaTreat every ablation as a measurement with noise. Change one thing, run several seeds per arm, use low-noise metrics (cloze formats, likelihoods, averaged checkpoints), and only trust differences larger than the seed noise. Choose benchmarks with a high signal-to-noise ratio, check that small-scale rankings agree with larger scale, and search hyperparameters with random search and early stopping rather than grids.

2 · Limitation

Three ways ablations mislead

Gap within noise

One seed per arm cannot separate a 0.9-point effect from a 0.57-point seed standard deviation.

Metric near chance

Small models score about 25% on 4-choice MMLU for much of training, so the score barely moves with real improvements.

Cherry-picking

Scanning up to 10,000 seeds on CIFAR-10, Picard found it "surprisingly easy" to find a much better or much worse outlier: report the distribution, not the best run.

3 · Your turn

Is filter B better?

Pick an answer before moving on. Committing to a guess, even a wrong one, makes the real answer stick.

Filter B beats filter A by 0.9 MMLU points, one run each; the seed standard deviation is 0.57 points. What can you conclude?

B. The difference of two single runs has standard error σ√2 ≈ 0.81 points; detecting a gap with 80% power at 5% significance needs about 2.8 × 0.81 ≈ 2.3 points. Add seeds or use a lower-noise metric.
4 · Core idea

Make the effect beat the noise

One change, several seeds

Hold everything else fixed; run 2-3 or more seeds per arm and compare means (paired where possible: same data order, same eval set).

Low-noise metrics

Cloze formats instead of multiple choice: MMLU-Cloze had seed σ 0.22 vs 0.57 and was far more monotonic during training. Likelihood or perplexity metrics instead of accuracy.

High signal-to-noise benchmarks

Signal: how far apart models of similar compute score. Noise: how much one run's score jitters across its final checkpoints. Benchmarks with higher SNR (signal-to-noise ratio) give more reliable small-scale decisions (R = 0.79).

Validate the proxy scale

DataDecide (25 corpora, models to 1B, 3 seeds): rankings at 150M predicted the 1B winner in about 80% of comparisons; no scaling-law method of 8 baselines did better for the same compute.

5 · How it works

Searching hyperparameters

Grid search

Wastes trials: most hyperparameters barely matter, so a grid re-tests the same values of the ones that do.

Random search

Bergstra and Bengio: randomly chosen trials are more efficient than a grid, because "only a few of the hyper-parameters really matter", and which ones differ by dataset.

Early stopping (Hyperband)

Start many random configurations cheaply and give more compute only to the promising ones: over an order-of-magnitude speedup over the Bayesian optimisation baselines tested.

Record everything that defines a run (code commit, data version, config, seed) so that any result can be reproduced and compared.

5 · How it works

Worked example: is the new data filter better?

Two 7B-architecture runs differ only in a data filter. MMLU (multiple choice) improves by 0.9 points. Meta measured a seed standard deviation of 0.57 points for this setup (0.22 for MMLU-Cloze).

Worked example

  1. One seed per arm: the difference has SE (standard error) = 0.57 × √2 = 0.81, so the smallest reliably detectable gap is 2.80 × 0.81 = 2.3 points.
  2. 0.9 < 2.3: the result is within seed noise. It is not evidence either way.
  3. Switch to the cloze format (σ = 0.22): with 3 seeds per arm the detectable gap is 2.80 × 0.22 × √(2/3) = 0.50 points.
  4. Re-run 3 seeds per arm; a 0.9-point cloze gain would now be a real signal.
Size the experiment before running it: seeds and metric choice decide what you can detect.
6 · Why it works

Why this matters

Compute is the budget

Every extra seed or ablation costs compute; sizing experiments decides how many questions the budget can answer.

Errors compound

A wrong small-scale decision is baked into a large run that costs orders of magnitude more.

Metrics change with scale

Benchmarks can sit near chance at small scale and become informative only later; continuous metrics made benchmarks over 80% predictable at 1B with 0.01% of the compute in DataDecide.

Averaging helps

Averaging the scores of a run's final checkpoints raised decision accuracy from 68.9% to 71.3% on a 30-task average (Signal and Noise).

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"A bigger benchmark means no need for multiple seeds."

More questions shrink question-sampling error, not training noise: the seed changes the model itself. You need both kinds of error under control.

"If the small model wins, the large one will too."

Usually, not always: about 80% of pairwise comparisons transferred from 150M to 1B in DataDecide. Validate the proxy for your setting.

"Grid search is the safe, thorough choice."

It spends trials on unimportant hyperparameters; random search covers the important ones better for the same budget.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • Detectable gap ≈ 2.8 · σ · √(2/n). n seeds per arm; quadruple the seeds to halve the gap.
  • Prefer continuous metrics at small scale. Likelihood or cloze versions of benchmarks have far less noise than accuracy on multiple choice.
  • Check small-to-large agreement. Validate that your proxy scale ranks recipes the way the target scale does.

⚠ Traps

  • One seed per arm. Many ablation gaps are smaller than seed noise.
  • Benchmarks near chance. Small models score around 25% on 4-choice MMLU: the metric carries almost no signal.
  • Picking the best checkpoint or seed. Selecting the maximum of noisy scores reports luck as progress.
8 · Check yourself

Check yourself

Answer from memory. If you can't, revisit the slide named in the answer.

You want to halve the smallest gap you can detect. How many seeds per arm do you need, relative to now?

B. The detectable gap scales with 1/√n, so halving it takes 4× the seeds (or a metric with half the noise).

Why do cloze formulations help pretraining ablations?

Small models score near chance on multiple-choice letter formats; cloze scoring of the answer text gives a smoother, more monotonic signal with lower seed variance (MMLU-Cloze σ 0.22 vs 0.57).

What is signal-to-noise ratio for a benchmark?

Signal: the spread of scores between models of similar compute (relative dispersion). Noise: the relative standard deviation of one run over its final checkpoints. High SNR predicts reliable small-to-large decisions.
9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

An ablation is a measurement: if the gap is smaller than the seed noise, you learned nothing.
Say it in 30 seconds

Labs choose data, architecture and hyperparameters from small runs. Each run's score varies with the seed, by about 0.2 to 0.8 points on common benchmarks at 7B. So run several seeds, prefer low-noise metrics like cloze formats or likelihoods, pick benchmarks with a high signal-to-noise ratio, confirm that the small scale ranks recipes like the target scale (DataDecide: about 80% of comparisons from 150M to 1B), and search hyperparameters randomly with early stopping.

Noise floor: seed variance sets the smallest effect you can see
Metric: cloze and likelihood metrics are less noisy
Scale: check that small runs rank recipes like large ones
Search: random search + early stopping beats grids

Further reading

Key papers

The primary sources behind this deck, each with what it contributed. Links go to the paper (arXiv or the conference page).

Quantifying Variance in Evaluation Benchmarks
Madaan et al. · arXiv 2024

Ten Llama-2-7B-architecture models trained for 210B tokens that differ only in seed: seed standard deviation ARC-C 0.80, HellaSwag 0.21, GSM8K (Grade School Math 8K) 0.41, MMLU 0.57, MMLU-Cloze 0.22; gains should exceed seed variance; use cloze formulations for pretraining ablations.

Signal and Noise: A Framework for Reducing Uncertainty in Language Model Evaluation
Heineman et al. · arXiv 2025

Signal (relative dispersion of scores across models of similar compute) over noise (relative std of one run's final checkpoints) predicts small-to-large decision accuracy; perplexity beats accuracy; filtering noisy subtasks and averaging checkpoints help. 30 benchmarks, 375 models from 60M to 32B.

Remember: SNR vs decision accuracy R = 0.791; checkpoint averaging 68.9% → 71.3% decision accuracy (30-task average)
DataDecide: How to Predict Best Pretraining Data with Small Experiments
Magnusson et al. · arXiv 2025

Controlled pretraining over 25 corpora, up to 100B tokens and 1B parameters, 3 seeds: rankings at a single small scale are a strong baseline; no scaling-law method among 8 baselines beats it for the same compute; continuous likelihood metrics make benchmarks highly predictable.

Remember: 150M rankings predict ~80% of 1B comparisons; >80% predictable with 0.01% of the compute
Random Search for Hyper-Parameter Optimization
Bergstra & Bengio · JMLR 2012

Randomly chosen trials are more efficient than a grid because only a few hyperparameters really matter, and which ones differ by dataset.

Hyperband: A Novel Bandit-Based Approach to Hyperparameter Optimization
Li et al. · JMLR 2018

Speeds up random search with adaptive resource allocation and early stopping, framed as a pure-exploration bandit problem.

Remember: over an order-of-magnitude speedup over the Bayesian-optimisation competitor set
Torch.manual_seed(3407) is all you need: On the influence of random seeds in deep learning architectures for computer vision
Picard · arXiv 2021

Scanning up to 10^4 seeds on CIFAR-10 (and fewer on ImageNet) shows it is surprisingly easy to find outlier seeds that perform much better or worse than average.

Adding Error Bars to Evals: A Statistical Approach to Language Model Evaluations
Miller · arXiv 2024

Standard errors from the central limit theorem, clustered standard errors, paired comparisons and power analysis for evals.

Remember: clustered SEs can be over 3× naive ones
