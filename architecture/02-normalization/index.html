Pre-norm and RMSNorm

Where the normalization sits in a residual block decides whether a deep Transformer trains smoothly. And most of what LayerNorm computes turns out to be unnecessary.

What is the problem?Deep post-norm Transformers need a carefully tuned learning-rate warm-up or they diverge, and LayerNorm itself costs time on every token in every layer.
Core ideaPre-norm: normalize the input of each sub-layer, x + F(Norm(x)), so the residual path is a clean identity. RMSNorm: skip the mean subtraction and just divide by the root mean square.
1 · Problem

Two ways to wire the same block

A Transformer layer is a sub-layer F (attention or FFN) plus a residual connection plus a normalization. The 2017 paper puts the norm after the addition. Switch between the two wirings and follow the main path (thick line) from input to output.

2 · Limitation

What goes wrong with post-norm

Large gradients near the output

Xiong et al. (2020) show with mean-field analysis that at initialization the gradients of parameters near the output layer are large in post-norm, and they don't shrink as you add layers.

Warm-up becomes mandatory

With large gradients, a normal learning rate makes the first updates too big. Post-norm models need a warm-up stage: start with a tiny learning rate and ramp up. That is one more sensitive hyperparameter, and it slows training.

LayerNorm does two jobs

LayerNorm re-centres (subtracts the mean) and re-scales (divides by the standard deviation), then applies a gain and a bias. That is two reductions per token per norm, twice per layer, on every token ever processed.

3 · Your turn

Where should the norm go?

Pick an answer before moving on. Committing to a guess, even a wrong one, makes the real answer stick.

You want the residual stream to carry each layer's output straight to the end of the network, unchanged. Which wiring achieves that?

B. In x + F(Norm(x)), the norm only touches the copy that goes into F. The x that is passed along is never rescaled, so after L layers the output is x₀ + F₁ + F₂ + … + F_L: a plain sum with an identity path from every layer to the end.
4 · Core idea

Two independent changes

Pre-norm: move the norm inside the branch

x ← x + Attn(Norm(x))
x ← x + FFN(Norm(x))
Then add one final norm before the output head, because the residual stream is never normalized along the way. GPT-2 did exactly this: "Layer normalization was moved to the input of each sub-block… and an additional layer normalization was added after the final self-attention block."

RMSNorm: keep only the re-scaling

RMSNorm(x) = g ⊙ x / √(mean(x²) + ε)
No mean subtraction, no bias. Zhang & Sennrich hypothesize that LayerNorm's re-centring invariance is "dispensable" and report comparable quality with 7–64% less running time across their models. LLaMA uses pre-norm with RMSNorm.

Why the final norm matters

In pre-norm, each layer adds to the residual stream, so its scale grows with depth (notebook 01 measures this). Each sub-layer still sees normalized input, but the logits would see the raw, growing stream. The final RMSNorm (model.norm in Llama code) fixes the scale once, just before the output head.

How to read it

Post-norm stays between about 165 and 210 however deep the stack: the gradient near the output does not shrink with depth, which is the paper's result for post-LN. With a normal learning rate those large updates destabilize early training, hence warm-up. Pre-norm falls roughly like 1/√L (Xiong et al. prove an O(d√(ln d / L)) scaling), so updates stay modest and warm-up can be dropped. In the paper, pre-LN Transformers trained without warm-up reached comparable results with significantly less training time and hyperparameter tuning.

5 · How it works

LayerNorm vs RMSNorm on a real vector

Eight features of one token. Shift them all up, or scale them all, and compare what the two norms output. (Gain g = 1, bias = 0.)

What the demo shows

  • Scaling the input changes neither output: both norms are re-scaling invariant.
  • Shifting the input changes RMSNorm's output but not LayerNorm's, because LayerNorm subtracts the mean first. RMSNorm gives up shift invariance, and the paper's experiments suggest models don't need it.
  • Cost: LayerNorm needs a mean and a variance per token; RMSNorm needs one sum of squares. On GPUs these are memory-bound reductions, so the saving is in passes over the data.
6 · Why it works

Why pre-norm trains more easily

An identity path to every layer

The output is x₀ plus the sum of every layer's contribution, so each layer receives gradient directly from the loss through the residual stream. That is the same idea as pre-activation ResNets, which GPT-2 cites.

Gradients well-behaved at initialization

Xiong et al. show pre-LN's gradients are well behaved at init, so the warm-up stage can be removed. Fewer hyperparameters, faster training.

The cost: a growing stream

Because nothing renormalizes the stream, its magnitude grows with depth. Later layers' contributions are relatively smaller, which is why a final norm is required and why some very deep models add extra tricks.

RMSNorm: a free speed-up

Same placement, fewer operations. Because it's a drop-in replacement with comparable quality, there is little reason not to use it, and LLaMA, Llama 2 and Llama 3 all do.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"LayerNorm normalizes across the batch, like BatchNorm."

No. Both LayerNorm and RMSNorm normalize each token's feature vector on its own. Nothing depends on other tokens or other sequences, which is why they work the same in training and at inference with any batch size.

"Pre-norm means you don't need a norm at the end."

The opposite. Because the residual stream is never normalized, pre-norm models need a final norm before the output head.

"RMSNorm is an approximation of LayerNorm that costs some quality."

It is a different normalization (no re-centring) that the authors found comparable in quality across tasks, and faster. LLaMA-family models use it.

Which formula is RMSNorm?

B. Divide by the root mean square and scale by a learned gain. No mean and no bias.

Why does post-norm usually need a learning-rate warm-up?

At initialization the gradients of parameters near the output are large (Xiong et al., 2020). A full learning rate on large gradients makes the first updates too big and training unstable; warm-up keeps early steps small.

h = x + Attention(RMSNorm(x)) and out = h + SwiGLU(RMSNorm(h)). After the last block: RMSNorm, then the output head.
9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

Put the norm inside the residual branch (pre-norm) and drop the mean (RMSNorm): stable deep training without warm-up, at lower cost.
Say it in 30 seconds

The 2017 Transformer normalizes after each residual addition. At initialization that makes gradients near the output large regardless of depth, so training needs a learning-rate warm-up. Pre-norm normalizes only the input to each sub-layer, x plus F of Norm x, so the residual stream is a clean sum with an identity path from every layer to the output; gradients are well behaved and warm-up can go. You then need one final norm before the output head. RMSNorm goes further and drops LayerNorm's mean subtraction, dividing only by the root mean square. Quality is comparable and it is cheaper. Llama uses both.

Problem: post-norm needs warm-up and LayerNorm costs two reductions per token.
Pre-norm: x + F(Norm(x)) plus a final norm; the gradient no longer stays large near the output.
RMSNorm: x / rms(x) · g; scale-invariant, not shift-invariant, 7–64% faster in the paper.
