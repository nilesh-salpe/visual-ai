robots.txt and consent

How websites tell crawlers what they may fetch, the exact matching rules standardised in RFC (Request for Comments) 9309, what to do when the file is missing or failing, and the fast-growing restrictions on AI (artificial intelligence) crawlers.

What is the problem?Site owners need a simple way to say "don't crawl this", and crawlers need unambiguous rules. Increasingly, sites also want to opt out of AI training specifically.
Core ideaA site serves /robots.txt with user-agent groups of allow/disallow rules. A crawler picks the group matching its product token (else *), and the longest matching rule wins (allow on ties). 4xx → crawl allowed; 5xx → assume disallow. Cache for at most 24 hours.

2 · Limitation

Why rules need to be precise

Ambiguity

Before RFC 9309, crawlers disagreed on first-match vs longest-match and on wildcards.

Failure modes

What should a crawler do when robots.txt returns 404, 403 or 500? Different answers mean very different behaviour.

New purposes

A file written for search indexing now also governs collection for AI training.

4 · Core idea

The rules in RFC 9309

Groups

One or more user-agent lines followed by rules. Match your product token case-insensitively; merge duplicate groups; fall back to *.

Matching

Paths match from the first character; * matches any sequence, $ anchors the end. Most octets wins; allow beats an equivalent disallow.

Errors

4xx: "unavailable", may crawl anything. 5xx or network failure: "unreachable", assume complete disallow.

Limits

Follow ≥ 5 redirects; cache ≤ 24 hours; parse at least 500 KiB.

At a glance

RFC 9309 (2022) standardised a 1994 convention.

5 · How it works

Consent is shifting fast

robots.txt restrictions

Longpre et al. (2024): in one year (2023-04 to 2024-04), about 5%+ of all tokens in C4, RefinedWeb and Dolma, and about 25%+ of tokens from the most critical domains, became restricted by robots.txt.

Terms of service

A full 45% of C4 is now restricted by terms-of-service crawling clauses.

Identifying crawlers

Crawlers announce a product token (e.g. CCBot) and can be blocked individually; Common Crawl documents how to block or slow CCBot.

Why this matters for data

If honoured, these restrictions bias the open web corpus toward sites that do not opt out, affecting its diversity and freshness, as the authors warn.

5 · How it works

Worked example: evaluating one URL

robots.txt: "User-agent: * / Disallow: /private/ / Allow: /private/public-page.html". Crawler: SomeBot. URL path: /private/public-page.html.

Worked example

  1. No group names SomeBot, so the * group applies.
  2. Both rules match: Disallow /private/ (9 characters) and Allow /private/public-page.html (25 characters).
  3. The longest match wins: allowed. For /private/x only the Disallow matches: disallowed.
  4. If the server answered robots.txt with 503 instead, the crawler must assume complete disallow; with 404, everything is allowed.
Specificity, not order, decides: the rule with the most matching characters wins.
6 · Why it works

Why the protocol works (and its limits)

Simple and universal

A text file at a fixed path that any crawler can fetch and parse.

Deterministic

Longest match and explicit tie-breaking give one answer per URL.

Fails safe on errors

Server errors mean "assume disallow", protecting struggling sites.

Voluntary

It is not access control: compliance depends on the crawler, and paths are public.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"The first matching rule applies."

RFC 9309 uses the most specific (longest) match; order does not matter.

"A missing robots.txt means "do not crawl"."

A 4xx response means the crawler may access everything; only server errors mean disallow.

"robots.txt protects private pages."

It is advisory and lists the paths publicly; use authentication for real protection.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • Longest match wins. Allow wins ties.
  • A specific group replaces *. Rules are not inherited from the * group.
  • Status codes. 4xx → allowed, 5xx/unreachable → disallow all.

⚠ Traps

  • First-match semantics. Order of lines does not matter in RFC 9309.
  • Treating robots.txt as security. It is advisory and publicly lists the paths.
  • Assuming Crawl-delay is standard. It is a non-standard extension (CCBot obeys it, others may not).

Assume complete disallow (unreachable).

It SHOULD NOT use a cached copy for more than 24 hours, unless the file is unreachable.

Yes: the matching ExampleBot group replaces the * group entirely.
9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

robots.txt groups rules by crawler; the longest matching rule wins (allow on ties), 4xx means allowed and 5xx means disallow, and AI-specific opt-outs are rising fast.
Say it in 30 seconds

Sites publish crawl rules in robots.txt as groups of allow and disallow lines for named crawlers. RFC 9309 fixes the details: a crawler uses the group naming its product token, otherwise the star group; paths match from the start with star and dollar wildcards; the longest match wins, and allow wins a tie. If fetching robots.txt gives a 4xx error the crawler may access everything, but a server error means assume everything is disallowed. Caches last at most a day. The file is voluntary, and audits show restrictions aimed at AI crawlers grew sharply, about five percent of C4 tokens in one year.
