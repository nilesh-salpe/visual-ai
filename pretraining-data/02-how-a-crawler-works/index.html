How a web crawler works

The loop at the heart of every crawler: a frontier of URLs to fetch, politeness per host, robots.txt, fetching, link extraction and duplicate checks.

What is the problem?Starting from a few seed URLs, we want to download billions of pages, never twice, without overloading any website, and while honouring its rules.
Core ideaKeep a URL frontier; repeatedly take a URL, check robots.txt, fetch it, extract and normalise its links, and add the unseen ones. Organise the frontier as one queue per host plus a heap of "next allowed contact" times, so each host is visited politely while thousands of others keep the crawler busy.

Threads, hosts and politeness

2 · Limitation

Why a simple loop isn't enough

Impolite

A FIFO (first in, first out) queue fetches whatever is next, often one site many times in a row, which can overload small servers.

Duplicates

The same page appears under many URLs (case, ports, fragments, tracking parameters).

Slow per host

Network round trips dominate; a single connection would crawl a few pages per second.

4 · Core idea

The crawl loop

Frontier

Front-end queues by priority; back-end FIFO queues, one per host; a heap of next-contact times (Najork & Heydon).

Fetch

Resolve DNS (cached), check robots.txt (cached ≤ 24 h), download with an identifying User-Agent.

Process

Extract links, normalise, drop already-seen URLs and pages whose content fingerprint was seen.

At a glance

Politeness lives in the frontier: a host is offered again only after its delay has passed.

5 · How it works

Inside Mercator's frontier

1 · prioritise

New URLs go to front-end FIFO queues by priority.

2 · route by host

A router moves URLs into back-end queues, each holding URLs of a single host.

3 · wait politely

A heap orders back-end queues by the time their host may next be contacted.

4 · fetch and reschedule

After a download, reinsert the queue at now + 10 × download time.

The original Google crawler

Brin & Page (1998): a URL server sends lists of URLs to several crawlers; each keeps about 300 connections open, and four crawlers together reached over 100 pages per second, with a DNS cache per crawler.

5 · How it works

Worked example: politeness and throughput

Mercator waited 10× the last download time before contacting a host again. Suppose a page takes 0.5 s to download and you want 1,000 pages/s.

Worked example

  1. One host can then be fetched at most once every 0.5 × (1 + 10) = 5.5 s: 0.18 pages/s per host.
  2. To reach 1,000 pages/s you need at least 1,000 ÷ 0.18 ≈ 5,500 hosts in flight at once.
  3. With 0.5 s per download, 1,000 pages/s also needs about 500 concurrent connections (threads or async requests).
  4. Mercator kept about 3 back-end host queues per crawling thread so threads never wait for a polite host.
Politeness caps per-host speed, so throughput comes from parallelism across many hosts, not from hammering one.
6 · Why it works

Why this design works

Polite by construction

A host cannot be offered again before its delay expires, whatever the link structure.

Fast in aggregate

Thousands of hosts in flight hide each host's waiting time.

Priorities

Front-end queues let important or fresh pages go first.

No wasted fetches

Normalisation and seen-sets keep each page to one download.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"A crawler just follows links recursively."

Scheduling (priorities, per-host politeness, revisits) is the hard part, not following links.

"Faster crawling means more requests per site."

Large crawlers get faster by covering more sites in parallel, not by hitting each harder.

"robots.txt is checked once per crawl."

It is cached (RFC (Request for Comments) 9309: no more than 24 hours) and re-fetched, and its status code matters.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • One queue per host. The heap holds when each host may be contacted next.
  • Normalise URLs. Lowercase host, drop default port and #fragment.
  • Cache DNS. Brin & Page found DNS lookup a major bottleneck.

⚠ Traps

  • Plain FIFO frontier. Hits the same host back to back (notebook 01: 0 s gaps).
  • Ignoring robots.txt status codes. 5xx means assume complete disallow.
  • Re-fetching duplicates. Without a seen-set, link-rich sites dominate the crawl.

9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

A crawler loops frontier → robots check → fetch → extract and normalise links → seen-set, with per-host queues and next-contact times that keep it polite while thousands of hosts keep it fast.
Say it in 30 seconds

A web crawler starts from seed URLs and repeats a loop: take the next URL from the frontier, check the site's robots.txt, resolve the host and download the page, extract its links, normalise them and add any it has not seen. Politeness is built into the frontier: Mercator kept one queue per host and a heap of times when each host may be contacted again, waiting ten times the last download time. That caps each site at a gentle rate, so throughput comes from crawling thousands of hosts in parallel. Normalisation and fingerprints stop the same page being fetched twice.

Problem: billions of pages, fetched once, politely.
Core idea: per-host queues + heap of next-contact times.
Throughput: many hosts × many connections.

Key papers

High-Performance Web Crawling (Mercator)
Najork & Heydon · Compaq SRC (Systems Research Center) Research Report 173 2001

Mercator's URL frontier: front-end priority queues, back-end per-host FIFO queues and a heap of next-contact times for politeness; content-seen and URL-seen tests with fingerprints.

Remember: wait 10× the last download time before re-contacting a host
The Anatomy of a Large-Scale Hypertextual Web Search Engine
Brin & Page · WWW (World Wide Web (conference)) 1998

The original Google: a URL server feeding distributed crawlers, a store server and an indexer; DNS caching was a major crawler bottleneck.

Remember: 4 crawlers × ~300 connections, 100+ pages/s
RFC 9309: Robots Exclusion Protocol
Koster, Illyes, Zeller & Sassman · IETF (Internet Engineering Task Force) 2022

Standardizes robots.txt: user-agent groups, allow/disallow with * and $, longest match wins (allow on ties), 4xx means crawl allowed, 5xx means assume complete disallow.

Remember: cache ≤ 24 h; parse ≥ 500 KiB
