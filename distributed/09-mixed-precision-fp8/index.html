Mixed precision and FP8 training

Training in 16-bit and 8-bit formats without losing accuracy: FP32 (32-bit floating point) master weights, loss scaling for FP16 (16-bit floating point), why BF16 (bfloat16, a 16-bit floating-point format with the exponent range of 32-bit floats) usually needs none, and the fine-grained scaling FP8 (8-bit floating point) training needs.

What is the problem?Low-precision math is 2–4× faster and halves memory, but small gradients underflow to zero in FP16 and FP8 has very few bits of precision and range.
Core ideaKeep an FP32 master copy of the weights and accumulate in FP32. For FP16, scale the loss so gradients stay representable. BF16 has FP32's range, so it usually needs no scaling. FP8 needs per-block scales (DeepSeek-V3: 1×128 tiles for activations, 128×128 blocks for weights).

2 · Limitation

What goes wrong in low precision

Underflow

FP16's smallest subnormal is about 6 × 10⁻⁸; many gradients are smaller and become exactly zero.

Lost updates

Adding a tiny update to a large weight in 16-bit rounds it away; the weight never changes.

Outliers

With 8 bits, one large value sets the scale for a whole tensor and pushes small values to zero.

3 · Your turn

Why keep FP32 weights?

Pick an answer before moving on. Committing to a guess, even a wrong one, makes the real answer stick.

A BF16 weight of 1.0 receives an update of 10⁻⁴. What happens if you add it directly in BF16?

B. BF16 has 7 mantissa bits, so values near 1 are spaced 2⁻⁷ ≈ 0.0078 apart; 1 + 10⁻⁴ rounds back to 1. Keeping an FP32 master copy accumulates small updates correctly.
4 · Core idea

The mixed-precision recipe

FP32 master weights

The optimizer updates an FP32 copy; a low-precision copy is used for compute (Micikevicius et al., 2018).

Loss scaling (FP16)

Multiply the loss by S before backward, divide gradients by S before the update; adjust S dynamically.

BF16 by default

Same range as FP32, so no loss scaling in most LLM (large language model) training; FP32 accumulation for reductions.

FP8 with fine-grained scales

DeepSeek-V3: activations scaled per 1×128 tile (per token per 128 channels), weights per 128×128 block, with increased-precision accumulation.

At a glance

Low-precision math, high-precision bookkeeping.

5 · How it works

Dynamic loss scaling, step by step

1 · scale

Multiply the loss by S (e.g. 2¹⁵) before backward.

2 · check

If any gradient is inf or NaN (not a number), skip the step and halve S.

3 · unscale

Otherwise divide gradients by S in FP32 and update the master weights.

4 · grow

After N clean steps, double S to keep using the available range.

What notebook 06 shows

On a toy problem with tiny features, FP16 gradients without scaling mostly flush to zero and training stalls at about a third of the starting loss; with dynamic scaling, training matches FP32. Casting a realistic spread of gradient values to FP16 keeps 37% of them non-zero without scaling and 100% with a 2¹⁵ scale or with BF16.

5 · How it works

Worked example: what each format can hold

Largest and smallest normal values (subnormals extend the small end a little).

Worked example

  1. FP32: max ≈ 3.4 × 10³⁸, smallest normal ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻³⁸, 23 mantissa bits.
  2. BF16: same exponent range as FP32, only 7 mantissa bits (≈ 2–3 decimal digits).
  3. FP16: max 65,504, smallest normal ≈ 6.1 × 10⁻⁵ (subnormals down to ≈ 6 × 10⁻⁸), 10 mantissa bits. A gradient of 10⁻⁸ becomes 0.
  4. FP8 E4M3: max 448; E5M2: max 57,344. With so little range, one scale per tensor is not enough, hence per-tile scaling.
FP16 needs loss scaling to protect tiny gradients; BF16 trades precision for FP32's range; FP8 needs scales per small block.
6 · Why it works

Why low precision is worth the trouble

Speed

Tensor Cores run BF16 at twice FP32-TF32 rates and FP8 at twice BF16 (H100: 989 vs 1,979 TFLOP (trillion floating-point operations)/s dense).

Memory and bandwidth

Half the bytes for activations and communication; Micikevicius et al. report nearly 2× less memory.

Accuracy holds

With master weights, FP32 accumulation and scaling, low-precision training matches FP32; DeepSeek-V3 trained in FP8 at 671B scale.

Stability tools

FP32 reductions (Llama 3), skipping overflow steps, and fine-grained scales keep training stable.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"BF16 is more accurate than FP16."

BF16 has more range but less precision (7 vs 10 mantissa bits); it is preferred because range problems are harder to fix than precision.

"Mixed precision means everything is 16-bit."

Master weights, optimizer states and many reductions stay FP32.

"FP8 training is just FP8 casting."

It needs fine-grained scaling, higher-precision accumulation and careful choice of which ops stay in BF16.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • Master weights in FP32. Small updates vanish if added directly to BF16 weights.
  • Dynamic loss scaling. Halve on overflow and skip the step; double after N good steps.
  • Accumulate in FP32. Llama 3 accumulates and reduce-scatters gradients in FP32.

⚠ Traps

  • FP16 without loss scaling. Small gradients flush to zero and training stalls.
  • BF16 weight updates. lr × gradient is often below BF16's resolution at the weight's magnitude.
  • Per-tensor FP8 scales. One outlier forces a scale that crushes the rest; use fine-grained blocks.

9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

Mixed precision computes in BF16/FP8 but keeps FP32 master weights and accumulations; FP16 needs loss scaling, FP8 needs fine-grained scales.
Say it in 30 seconds

Low-precision math is faster and halves memory and communication, but it loses range and precision. Mixed precision training keeps an FP32 master copy of the weights and accumulates gradients in FP32, while matmuls run in 16 or 8 bits. FP16's narrow range makes small gradients underflow, so the loss is scaled up before backward and the gradients scaled down before the update, with dynamic scaling that halves the scale and skips the step on overflow. BF16 has FP32's exponent range, so it usually needs no scaling. FP8 has so few bits that DeepSeek-V3 scales activations per 1 by 128 tile and weights per 128 by 128 block.
