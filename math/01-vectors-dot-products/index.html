Vectors and dot products

The single operation behind embeddings, similarity search and attention: multiply matching coordinates and add them up.

What is the problem?A language model represents every token as a list of thousands of numbers. To use them it must constantly answer one question: how related are these two lists?
Core ideaThe dot product a·b = Σ aᵢbᵢ = |a||b|cos θ measures alignment scaled by length. Cosine similarity removes the lengths. Attention scores are dot products of queries and keys, divided by √d to keep their spread near 1.

2 · Limitation

Why not compare vectors some other way?

Coordinate by coordinate

Checking whether entries match one by one says nothing about overall alignment in thousands of dimensions.

Euclidean distance

Useful, but for unit vectors |a − b|² = 2 − 2 a·b, so it is the dot product in disguise.

It must be fast

A model computes billions of comparisons per step; the dot product is one multiply-add per coordinate and maps perfectly onto matrix hardware.

4 · Core idea

Dot product, length and angle

Dot product

a·b = Σ aᵢbᵢ. Large when the vectors point the same way and are long.

Length (norm)

|a| = √(a·a). Dividing by it gives a unit vector that keeps only direction.

Cosine similarity

cos θ = a·b / (|a||b|), between −1 and 1. Used for embedding search and retrieval.

a · b = Σᵢ aᵢ bᵢ = |a| |b| cos θ

At a glance

Dot products appear in every layer: q·k for attention, h·eᵢ for the output logits.

5 · How it works

Worked example: similarity and the √d scale

Two 2-D vectors a = (3, 4) and b = (4, 3); then random 128-dimensional query and key vectors with mean-0, variance-1 entries.

Worked example

  1. a · b = 3·4 + 4·3 = 24; |a| = |b| = 5.
  2. cos θ = 24 / 25 = 0.96, so θ ≈ 16.3°: nearly the same direction.
  3. For random q, k in d = 128: q·k has mean 0 and variance d, so its standard deviation is √128 ≈ 11.3.
  4. Dividing by √d brings that back to about 1, so the softmax over scores is neither flat nor one-hot at initialisation.
The dot product mixes direction and length; cosine isolates direction; attention divides by √d to control scale.
6 · Why it works

Why the dot product is the right tool

It is a projection

a·b/|b| is the length of a's shadow on b: how much of a lies along b's direction.

It is linear

Dot products compose into matrix multiplications, which GPUs (graphics processing units) run at hundreds of TFLOP (trillion floating-point operations)/s (tera floating-point operations per second).

High dimensions help

Random directions are nearly orthogonal, so thousands of features can share one vector with little interference.

Scale is controllable

Dividing by √d or normalising vectors keeps magnitudes in a sensible range.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"A dot product of 0 means the vectors are opposite."

It means orthogonal: neither aligned nor opposed. Opposite vectors have cosine −1.

"Bigger dot product always means more similar."

Only for vectors of fixed length; otherwise length dominates. Use cosine to compare directions.

"The √d in attention is a learned parameter."

It is a fixed constant from the variance argument: d independent unit-variance products sum to variance d.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • a·b = |a||b|cos θ. Positive = aligned, 0 = orthogonal, negative = opposed.
  • Normalise once, then matmul. All cosines at once, as in vector search.
  • Random high-dimensional vectors are nearly orthogonal. Cosine spread ≈ 1/√d.

⚠ Traps

  • Comparing raw dot products across vectors of different lengths. Long vectors win regardless of direction.
  • Forgetting the √d. Unscaled attention scores have spread √d and saturate the softmax.
  • Treating cosine 0 as "opposite". 0 means unrelated (orthogonal); −1 is opposite.

Check yourself

cos θ for a = (1, 2, 2) and b = (2, 1, −2)?

1·2 + 2·1 + 2·(−2) = 0, so 0: orthogonal.

Why does attention divide q·k by √d?

For unit-variance entries, q·k has variance d. Dividing by √d keeps the scores' spread near 1 so the softmax does not saturate.

How do you find the 5 nearest embeddings to a query efficiently?

Normalise all rows once, compute one matrix-vector product for all cosines, take the top 5 (or use an approximate nearest-neighbour index).
9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

The dot product Σ aᵢbᵢ = |a||b|cos θ measures alignment; cosine removes length, and attention divides by √d to keep scores well scaled.
Say it in 30 seconds

Language models represent tokens as long vectors, and the dot product is how they compare them: multiply matching coordinates and add. It equals the two lengths times the cosine of the angle, so it is large when vectors point the same way. Cosine similarity divides out the lengths and is used for embedding search. Attention scores are dot products of queries and keys; because random vectors in d dimensions give dot products with spread √d, scores are divided by √d before the softmax.
