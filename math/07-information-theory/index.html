Math for LLMs · 07

Information theory for LLMs

Entropy, cross-entropy and KL (Kullback-Leibler divergence) divergence: the quantities behind the training loss, distillation, RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback)'s KL penalty and bits-per-byte comparisons.

What is the problem?LLM (large language model) work constantly asks "how different are these two distributions?": model vs data, student vs teacher, fine-tuned policy vs reference. We need a principled measure.
Core ideaEntropy H(p) is average surprise. Cross-entropy H(p, q) is the surprise when you code p's outcomes with model q. Their gap is the KL divergence KL(p‖q) = H(p, q) − H(p) ≥ 0: zero only when q = p, and not symmetric.

2 · Limitation

Why not simpler distances?

Difference of probabilities

Treats 0.01 vs 0.001 like 0.51 vs 0.501, though the first is a 10× difference in surprise.

Only the top outcome

Ignores how the rest of the probability is spread, which matters for sampling and distillation.

Needs to match training

We want the measure that maximum likelihood already minimises, so the numbers line up with the loss.

4 · Core idea

Entropy, cross-entropy, KL

Entropy H(p)

−Σ p log p. Average surprise of the data itself: the floor no model can beat.

Cross-entropy H(p, q)

−Σ p log q. Average surprise when predicting p's outcomes with q: the LM (language model) training loss.

KL(p‖q)

Σ p log(p/q) = H(p, q) − H(p). The excess caused by q ≠ p.

H(p, q) = H(p) + KL(p ‖ q), KL ≥ 0

At a glance

Training lowers KL(p‖q); H(p) is fixed by the data.

5 · How it works

Where KL appears in LLM work

DPO (direct preference optimization)implicit KL constraint in its derivationsame role without explicit RL

Forward vs reverse KL

Minimising KL(p‖q) (maximum likelihood) forces q to put mass wherever p has mass: mass-covering. Minimising KL(q‖p) lets q ignore parts of p but punishes mass where p has none: mode-seeking. Notebook 04 fits one bump to a two-peaked p both ways.

5 · How it works

Worked example: three outcomes

Data distribution p = (0.7, 0.2, 0.1); model q = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2).

Worked example

  1. H(p) = −Σ p log p ≈ 0.802 nats (1.157 bits).
  2. H(p, q) = −Σ p log q ≈ 0.887 nats.
  3. KL(p‖q) = H(p, q) − H(p) ≈ 0.085 nats.
  4. KL(q‖p) ≈ 0.092 nats: different, because KL is not symmetric.
The model pays 0.085 extra nats per outcome for being wrong; that excess is all that training can remove.
6 · Why it works

Why these are the right quantities

Compression

Shannon: entropy is the minimum average code length; cross-entropy is the length when coding with q. A better LM is a better compressor.

Consistency

The training loss, the evaluation metric (perplexity) and regularisers (KL penalties) share one currency.

Directional on purpose

The asymmetry of KL lets you choose coverage or mode-seeking behaviour.

Units

Nats with ln, bits with log₂; bits per byte removes tokenizer effects.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"KL divergence is a distance."

It is not symmetric and does not satisfy the triangle inequality, so it is a divergence, not a metric.

"Cross-entropy loss can go below the entropy of the data."

Not on the true distribution: H(p, q) ≥ H(p). A training loss below it signals overfitting or leaked test data.

"Entropy measures how good a model is."

Entropy of the model's own output measures its uncertainty; quality is cross-entropy against real data.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • H(p, q) = H(p) + KL(p‖q). Minimising cross-entropy minimises KL.
  • Forward KL is mass-covering. KL(p‖q) spreads q over all of p; reverse KL(q‖p) is mode-seeking.
  • Bits per byte = loss_nats × tokens / (bytes × ln 2). Tokenizer-independent.

⚠ Traps

  • Treating KL as a distance. It is asymmetric and violates the triangle inequality.
  • q = 0 where p > 0. KL becomes infinite; models must never assign exactly zero.
  • Mixing nats and bits. 1 nat = 1/ln 2 ≈ 1.443 bits.

9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

Entropy is average surprise, cross-entropy is surprise under a model, and KL = H(p, q) − H(p) ≥ 0 is the asymmetric gap that training, distillation and RLHF penalties control.
Say it in 30 seconds

Entropy measures the average surprise of a distribution; for data it is the floor no model can beat. Cross-entropy measures the surprise when you predict that data with a model, and it is exactly the language-model training loss. The difference is the KL divergence, which is never negative and is zero only when the model matches the data. So minimising the loss minimises KL from data to model, which is mass-covering. KL is not symmetric: the reverse direction is mode-seeking. The same quantities appear as distillation targets and as the KL penalty that keeps an RLHF policy near its reference.

Further reading

Key papers

The primary sources behind this deck, each with what it contributed. Links go to the paper (arXiv or the conference page).

A Mathematical Theory of Communication
Shannon · Bell System Technical Journal 1948

Defines entropy as average information and shows it is the limit of lossless compression; the root of cross-entropy loss and bits-per-token.

On Information and Sufficiency
Kullback & Leibler · Annals of Mathematical Statistics 1951

Introduces the divergence now called KL: the expected extra information needed when using one distribution in place of another.

Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network
Hinton, Vinyals & Dean · NeurIPS workshop 2015

Train a small model on a large model's softened output distribution (softmax with temperature T), minimizing cross-entropy to those soft targets.
