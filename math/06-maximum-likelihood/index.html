Math for LLMs · 06

Maximum likelihood and cross-entropy

Why the LLM (large language model) training loss is the average negative log-probability of the actual next token, what its values mean, and how it relates to perplexity.

What is the problem?We have a model that outputs next-token probabilities and a mountain of text. We need a single number that says how good the model is and that gradient descent can minimise.
Core ideaPick parameters that make the observed text most probable: maximum likelihood. Taking logs and averaging gives the cross-entropy loss L = −(1/N) Σ log p(xₜ | x₍<t₎). Its gradient with respect to the logits is simply p − onehot(target).
1 · Problem

How much should a wrong guess cost?

Move the probability the model gave to the actual next token. The curve is −log p: near-free when the model is confident and right, very expensive when it is confident and wrong.

2 · Limitation

Why not simpler losses?

Accuracy

Counting top-1 hits ignores how much probability went to the right token and has zero gradient almost everywhere.

Squared error on probabilities

Barely penalises assigning 0.001 to the right answer; gradients vanish when the model is confidently wrong.

Raw likelihood

The product of millions of probabilities underflows to 0; logs turn it into a sum.

3 · Your turn

Which costs more?

Pick an answer before moving on. Committing to a guess, even a wrong one, makes the real answer stick.

Which prediction gets the larger cross-entropy loss?

C. −ln 0.01 ≈ 4.6 nats, versus 0.69 for 0.5 and 0.11 for 0.9. Low probability on what actually happened is punished hardest.
4 · Core idea

Maximise likelihood = minimise cross-entropy

Likelihood

The probability the model assigns to the training text: Πₜ p(xₜ | x₍<t₎).

Log and average

Maximising it equals minimising the average −log p: the cross-entropy between data and model.

Simple gradient

For softmax outputs, ∂L/∂zᵢ = pᵢ − 1[i = target]: push the target up, everything else down in proportion.

L = −(1/N) Σₜ log p_θ(xₜ | x₍<t₎) · ∂L/∂z = p − onehot(xₜ)

At a glance

perplexity = e^loss

5 · How it works

Worked example: reading loss values

Losses in nats (natural log) per token; GPT-2's vocabulary has 50,257 tokens.

Worked example

  1. A model that guesses uniformly: loss = ln 50,257 ≈ 10.82 nats, perplexity 50,257.
  2. If the actual token always gets probability 0.25: loss = −ln 0.25 ≈ 1.386, perplexity 4.
  3. A loss of 2.0 nats means perplexity e² ≈ 7.39: as uncertain as choosing among about 7 equally likely tokens.
  4. One confident mistake, p(actual) = 0.001, costs −ln 0.001 ≈ 6.9 nats: the log punishes confident errors hard.
Loss is average surprise in nats; perplexity e^loss is the equivalent number of equally likely choices.
6 · Why it works

Why maximum likelihood is the default

Proper

The loss is minimised exactly when the model's distribution equals the data distribution, so honest probabilities are rewarded.

Dense signal

Every token of every document gives a gradient, unlike sparse task labels.

Information-theoretic meaning

Cross-entropy in bits is the code length for compressing the text with the model (Shannon).

Same loss everywhere

Pretraining, supervised fine-tuning and distillation all use cross-entropy, just with different targets.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"Cross-entropy and log-loss are different things."

For one-hot targets they are the same: −log p(actual).

"Lower perplexity always means a better assistant."

It means better next-token prediction on that text; helpfulness also depends on post-training.

"The loss should go to zero."

Text is inherently uncertain; the minimum achievable loss is the data's entropy, well above 0.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • ∂L/∂logits = p − y. Softmax + cross-entropy has the simplest gradient in deep learning.
  • Perplexity = e^loss. Or 2^loss if the loss is in bits.
  • Average per token. So losses compare across batch and sequence sizes.

⚠ Traps

  • Comparing perplexities across tokenizers. Different vocabularies change the unit; compare bits per byte instead.
  • log(softmax(z)) naively. Use log-softmax (Softmax deck) to avoid log 0.
  • Reading loss 0 as the goal. Natural text has irreducible uncertainty; held-out loss never reaches 0.

9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

LLMs are trained by maximum likelihood: minimise the average −log p of each actual next token (cross-entropy), whose logit gradient is simply p − onehot.
Say it in 30 seconds

Maximum likelihood chooses parameters that make the training text as probable as possible. Because that probability is a product over tokens, we take logs and average, giving the cross-entropy loss: the mean negative log-probability of each actual next token, measured in nats. It punishes confident mistakes heavily and is minimised exactly when the model matches the data distribution. With a softmax output its gradient is just the predicted probabilities minus one at the target. Perplexity, e to the loss, is the equivalent number of equally likely choices.

Problem: one trainable number for "how good is the model".
Core idea: L = mean −log p(actual); gradient p − onehot.
Read it: perplexity = e^loss; uniform = ln V.
