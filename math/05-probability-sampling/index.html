Math for LLMs · 05

Probability and sampling

A language model is a conditional probability distribution over the next token. How logits become probabilities, and how temperature, top-k and top-p shape what gets generated.

What is the problem?The model outputs one score (logit) per vocabulary entry. Generating text requires turning those scores into a distribution and choosing a token, and the choice changes the text dramatically.
Core ideaA language model factorises text with the chain rule of probability: p(x₁…xₙ) = Π p(xₜ | x₍<t₎). Softmax turns logits into p(next | context). Temperature sharpens or flattens it; top-k and top-p (nucleus) sampling cut the unreliable tail.

2 · Limitation

Why not always pick the most likely token?

Degeneration

Holtzman et al. found that maximising likelihood when decoding produces bland and strangely repetitive text.

Human text is not maximal

People routinely choose tokens the model ranks below the top one.

The tail is unreliable

Pure sampling occasionally picks one of thousands of very unlikely tokens and derails the text.

4 · Core idea

A distribution over the next token

Chain rule of probability

p(x₁…xₙ) = Π p(xₜ | x₁…xₜ₋₁): a language model only needs next-token conditionals.

Softmax

p(i) = exp(zᵢ) / Σⱼ exp(zⱼ): positive, sums to 1, preserves the ranking of logits.

Temperature

Use zᵢ / T. T < 1 sharpens, T > 1 flattens.

Truncation

Top-k keeps the k best tokens; top-p keeps the smallest set whose probability reaches p. Then renormalise.

p(xₜ = i | x₍<t₎) = exp(zᵢ / T) / Σⱼ exp(zⱼ / T)

At a glance

The same model produces very different text depending on the decoding rule.

5 · How it works

Worked example: temperature and top-p on four logits

Logits (2.0, 1.0, 0.5, −1.0) for tokens A, B, C, D.

Worked example

  1. T = 1: softmax = (0.609, 0.224, 0.136, 0.030).
  2. T = 0.5 (logits doubled): (0.842, 0.114, 0.042, 0.002): almost always A.
  3. T = 2 (logits halved): (0.434, 0.263, 0.205, 0.097): much flatter.
  4. Top-p with p = 0.9 at T = 1: cumulative A, B, C = 0.970 ≥ 0.9, so D is dropped and A, B, C are renormalised.
Temperature changes the shape of the whole distribution; top-k and top-p remove the tail, then renormalise.
6 · Why it works

Why this framing works

Any text, one rule

Because of the chain rule, predicting the next token well is enough to model whole documents.

Trainable

Softmax probabilities are differentiable, so maximum likelihood can train them (next deck).

Controllable

Decoding knobs trade diversity against reliability without retraining.

Composable

Log-probabilities let you score, compare and rerank candidate outputs.

7 · Misconceptions

Common misconceptions

Each statement below is a mental model people often get wrong. Decide why it is wrong, then reveal.

"Temperature 0 and top-k = 1 are different."

Both pick the single most likely token (greedy decoding), up to tie-breaking.

"Higher temperature makes the model smarter or more creative."

It only flattens the distribution: more diverse, but more likely to choose poor tokens.

"Top-p keeps a fixed number of tokens."

The number varies with the model's confidence: one token when the top probability exceeds p, many when the distribution is flat.
7 · Tricks and traps

Tricks and traps

Rules of thumb that make this topic quick to reason about, and the mistakes people most often make with it.

✓ Tricks

  • Temperature divides logits. T → 0 is greedy; T = 1 is the model's distribution.
  • Top-p adapts the cut. Few tokens when the model is confident, many when it is not.
  • Log-probabilities add. Sequence log-prob = Σ log p(xₜ | x₍<t₎).

⚠ Traps

  • Greedy decoding for open-ended text. Maximising likelihood gives bland, repetitive output (Holtzman et al.).
  • Multiplying many probabilities. Underflows; sum logs instead.
  • Thinking T changes the model. It only changes decoding; training is unaffected.

Check yourself

Probabilities 0.5, 0.3, 0.15, 0.05. Which tokens does top-p = 0.8 keep?

0.5 + 0.3 = 0.8 reaches p: the first two.

How do you compute the probability of a whole sentence under a language model?

Multiply the next-token probabilities, or better, sum their logs: log p = Σₜ log p(xₜ | x₍<t₎).

What problem does nucleus sampling solve?

It avoids both degenerate greedy text and derailment by sampling from an unreliable tail, by keeping only the high-probability nucleus.
9 · Key takeaway

Key takeaway

An LLM (large language model) is a next-token distribution p(xₜ | x₍<t₎) from a softmax over logits; temperature reshapes it and top-k/top-p cut the tail before sampling.
Say it in 30 seconds

Thanks to the chain rule of probability, a model of whole texts only needs next-token conditionals. The network outputs a logit per vocabulary entry and a softmax turns them into probabilities. Decoding then chooses a token: dividing logits by a temperature below one sharpens the distribution and above one flattens it; top-k keeps the k most likely tokens and top-p keeps the smallest set reaching probability p, then renormalises. Greedy decoding tends to produce bland, repetitive text, which is why sampling with truncation is common.
